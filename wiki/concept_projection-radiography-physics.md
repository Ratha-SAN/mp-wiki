## Synthesis

**What this page covers.** IAEA *Diagnostic Radiology Physics: A Handbook for Teachers and Students* (2014), Chapter 6 (Projection Radiography), read in full — the geometry, scatter physics, and technique-optimization framework for standard 2-D projection X-ray imaging, building directly on [[concept_diagnostic-radiology-physics-fundamentals]] (interaction physics), [[concept_medical-image-quality-theory]] (contrast/noise/SNR formalism), and [[equipment_x-ray-tube-and-generator]] (the source hardware).

**Primary contrast and the scatter fraction.** For a detail of linear attenuation coefficient μ_d and thickness x_d embedded in background material μ_b, primary contrast is C_P = 1 − e^(−(μ_d−μ_b)x_d); contrast depends on differences in mass attenuation coefficient, density, or both, and — since μ falls with photon energy — is inversely related to kV, making kV the primary "contrast control" in screen-film imaging (digital systems can independently adjust display contrast). Scattered radiation degrades this: scatter fraction SF = E_s/(E_p+E_s) and scatter-to-primary ratio SPR = E_s/E_p are related by SF = (SPR⁻¹+1)⁻¹, and primary contrast in the presence of scatter becomes C_P = [1−e^(−(μ_d−μ_b)x_d)]/(1+SPR) — scatter is not merely "fog," it is the central quantity linking field size, patient thickness, and kV setting to achievable image contrast.

**Projection geometry effects.** Superposition (2-D representation of a 3-D object) causes loss of depth information and object ambiguity — a primary historical motivation for CT. Geometrical distortion includes magnification (image size scales with object-to-image-distance, OID, causing same-size objects at different depths to project at different sizes — an inherent ambiguity) and shape distortion/foreshortening for angled objects. The inverse square law (ISL) holds almost exactly beyond ~50 cm from the focal spot for an X-ray tube with attached collimator (only mammography's low-kV beams show measurable air-attenuation deviation from ISL). Increasing focus-to-image distance (FID) while proportionally increasing focus-to-skin distance (FSD) can reduce skin dose at fixed image-receptor kerma, but does not meaningfully reduce effective dose, since the required field-size increase (to avoid clinical-region cutoff) roughly cancels the ISL-driven kerma reduction in the dose-area product. Geometric unsharpness U_g = X_F·d_OID/d_FID = X_F(m−1)/m (X_F = focal spot size, m = magnification) trades directly against focal-spot size: smaller focal spots improve sharpness but force lower tube current (longer exposure, more motion-blur risk) due to the anode thermal-loading limits already discussed in [[equipment_x-ray-tube-and-generator]] — some evidence suggests focal spots smaller than currently typical would improve spatial resolution further, since the focal-spot MTF itself measurably affects system DQE. Magnification imaging (routine only in mammography, see forthcoming Ch.9 ingest; occasionally unavoidable in lateral hip/cervical-spine views) trades reduced scatter fraction (fewer scattered rays reach a receptor placed further from the patient) and thus reduced grid dependence (Bucky-factor-scaled dose reduction) against focal-spot-driven unsharpness, requiring a smaller focal spot than contact imaging to net out favourably.

**Contrast agents and dual-energy imaging.** Iodine (K-edge 33 keV) and barium (K-edge 37 keV) compounds substantially increase soft-tissue/vascular contrast via K-edge photoelectric enhancement; optimum kV settings are 60-70 kV for iodine and up to 80 kV for barium, chosen so the exit spectrum's photon energies cluster just above the relevant K-edge. Adverse contrast-media reactions are rare but occasionally serious or fatal — flagged directly by the source (UNVERIFIED beyond this general statement; no incidence figures given). Gold-nanoparticle-based contrast agents are noted as an emerging alternative with reduced toxicity. Dual-energy imaging acquires two spectrally-distinct images (different kV and/or filtration) and combines them with photoelectric/Compton-effect weightings to separate bone from soft tissue or air from soft tissue — the physical basis of dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), covered in the forthcoming Ch.10 ingest.

**Technique selection — the kV/mAs/dose relationship.** Image receptor dose is approximately linear in mAs and roughly proportional to kV² (patient entrance dose, accounting for beam hardening across the exam, is closer to kV³). From Rose's expression, the number of detected photons required per unit area to image a detail of size d and contrast C at SNR k (Rose criterion k≈5) is N = k²/(C²d²) — raising kV lowers required contrast-matched photon count (since noise requirements ease as contrast improves with... actually the reverse: contrast falls with kV, so this trade-off is real and directional) but also raises patient dose per unit receptor dose; the practical corollary is that images taken at low kV with high mAs, or high kV with low mAs, can look deceptively similar in receptor-dose terms while differing greatly in patient dose — the source explicitly names this failure mode "exposure creep." Empirical shortcuts: the "15% rule" (15% kV increase ≈ 2× receptor dose, so mAs may be halved to compensate), the "5% rule" (5% kV increase ≈ 30% dose increase), and the "10 kV rule" (in the 60-80 kV range, ±10 kV ≈ halving/doubling mAs). Manufacturer-specific "exposure index" systems let digital-imaging technologists target consistent receptor dose per body region (extremities/trunk/chest roughly mapping to screen-film-equivalent speeds of 100/200/400) independent of the direct visual density feedback screen-film systems provided.

**Scatter magnitude and dependencies.** Incoherent (Compton) scatter dominates over coherent scatter as a source of image-degrading scattered radiation (coherent is only ~19% of scatter at 50 kV, ~9% at 120 kV for a 10 cm patient). Scatter fraction rises with field size (saturating at large sizes) and with patient thickness (also saturating), is higher for soft tissue than bone (except in mammography), and — counterintuitively — shows little net dependence on photon energy at the receptor for efficient receptors, because the falling photoelectric-to-Compton cross-section ratio with energy is offset by increasing forward-scatter escape probability; inefficient (older, less absorbing) receptors do show scatter-fraction increase with energy. Scattered dose at 1 m from the patient is roughly 1/1000 of the entrance-surface primary-beam dose — a practical shielding rule of thumb directly relevant to [[protocol_NCRP-facility-shielding]]'s occupancy-factor framework, though that page's NCRP-147/151 figures were derived independently.

**Scatter reduction: antiscatter grids and alternatives.** A focused antiscatter grid (lead strips separated by low-Z interspace material, strip height:width ratio = grid ratio r = h/d) rejects scattered photons whose incidence angle exceeds tan⁻¹r while transmitting the primary beam; higher grid ratios reject more scatter but also more primary photons, requiring a dose increase quantified by the Bucky factor (exposure-with-grid ÷ exposure-without-grid = 1/T_total). A good grid removes 80-90% of scatter while transmitting ≥75% of primary; typical clinical ratios are 10:1 or 12:1 (Bucky factors ~1.7-1.8 for chest, ~3.5-3.8 for abdomen soft tissue per the handbook's worked table). Grids must oscillate during exposure to blur out grid-line artifacts (especially important for digital systems, where static grid lines can alias against the pixel matrix as Moiré patterns) and must be used at their correct focal distance to avoid grid cut-off (progressive primary-beam attenuation toward field edges from strip/beam-divergence mismatch) — five distinct misalignment modes are named (upside-down, laterally decentred, tilted, wrong focal distance, and combinations). Non-grid alternatives: collimation (smaller field directly reduces scatter fraction, per the field-size dependence above), compression (reduces patient thickness, hence scatter — also shortens required exposure and permits lower kV; routine in mammography), air gap technique (increased OID lets divergent scattered rays miss the receptor — the same magnification-imaging mechanism discussed above), and scanning-slit systems (fan-beam or multi-fan-beam pre-/post-patient collimation minimizing the irradiated area at any instant — effective scatter rejection without a grid, at the cost of longer exposure times, increased tube wear, and greater artifact susceptibility from imperfect collimator-motion synchronization).

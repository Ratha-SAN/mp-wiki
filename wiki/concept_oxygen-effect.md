## Synthesis

**Mechanism.** Molecular oxygen present within milliseconds of irradiation chemically "fixes" free-radical damage to DNA (the oxygen fixation hypothesis), making it permanent rather than chemically restituted by reducing agents such as thiols (raw/Radiation Biology- A Handbook for Teachers and Students.pdf, §2.2.7, §2.3.12). This is the chemical basis of oxygen's radiosensitizing action and underlies the direct/indirect-action framework in [[concept_radiation-interactions]]. Chemical detail: a damaged biomolecule radical (M·) formed by direct or water-mediated indirect action can either be restituted by hydrogen donors (M· + RSH → MH + RS·, e.g. glutathione, a cell's normal protective mechanism) or, if oxygen reaches the site first, "fixed" as a stable, no-longer-restitutable peroxyl species (M· + O₂ → MO₂·) — this is why hypoxia is protective (no oxygen to outcompete restitution) rather than merely "less sensitizing" (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.3 §3.21.4). Oxygen can also directly increase radical yield by intercepting an excited/polarized biomolecule before it can relax back to its ground state (Ch.3 §3.21.4.1).

**Oxygen enhancement ratio (OER).** OER = dose in hypoxia / dose in air to achieve equal biological effect. For X/γ-rays at doses >~3 Gy, OER is typically 2.5–3.3 across cell lines and tissues; it is reduced (dose-dependent) below 3 Gy, which is clinically relevant since fraction sizes are usually ≤2 Gy (§2.3.12, §3.10). OER declines toward 1 for high-LET radiation above ~200 keV/μm (§3.10). Radiosensitivity rises steeply between pO2 of 0 and ~20 mmHg and plateaus above ~25 mmHg; tissue is considered hypoxic below ~10 mmHg (§2.3.12).

**Tumour hypoxia.** Solid tumours develop chronic hypoxia (diffusion-limited, beyond ~70 μm from a capillary) and acute/fluctuating hypoxia (due to transient vascular flow changes), driven by abnormal, leaky tumour angiogenesis (§2.4.6, §3.10). Typical hypoxic fractions are 1–20% of tumour cells; hypoxic cells dominate the response to large single doses even when a small minority of the population (§2.4.6, §3.10). **Reoxygenation** of surviving hypoxic cells between fractions is one of the "five Rs" of fractionation (see [[concept_fractionation]]) and is a major reason fractionated treatment improves therapeutic ratio relative to single large doses (§3.12.4). Hypoxia also upregulates HIF-1-driven genes (VEGF, GLUT-1, carbonic anhydrase IX) that promote angiogenesis, altered metabolism, invasiveness, and resistance to apoptosis, and may select for p53-mutant, more aggressive clones (§3.10).

**Measurement.** Polarographic (Eppendorf) oxygen electrodes give direct microregional pO2 but are invasive; intrinsic markers (HIF-1α, GLUT-1, CA-IX) and extrinsic markers (pimonidazole, EF-5) allow retrospective/immunohistochemical assessment but have shown inconsistent correlation with each other, reflecting tumour hypoxia heterogeneity (§3.10, §3.13.1). Hypoxia measurements have shown predictive value for outcome (e.g. cervix, head and neck cancer) but are not yet used routinely to modify treatment (§3.13.1).

**Radiosensitizers and radioprotectors.** Hypoxic-cell radiosensitizers (nitroimidazoles: misonidazole, etanidazole, nimorazole) mimic oxygen's radiosensitizing effect; clinical benefit has been inconsistent trial-by-trial but a meta-analysis of >7000 patients across 50 trials showed a small significant benefit, concentrated in head and neck cancer (§3.14). Bioreductive cytotoxins (tirapazamine, AQ4N) are selectively toxic under hypoxia (§3.14). Radioprotectors (amifostine/WR-2721, cysteine, cysteamine) donate hydrogen atoms to restitute radical damage and localize preferentially in normal tissue via alkaline phosphatase activation, offering some normal-tissue protection with debated tumour-protection risk (§2.3.13, §3.14). Other radiosensitizing strategies include halogenated pyrimidines (BrdU/IdU), EGFR inhibitors (cetuximab), and DNA-repair/checkpoint inhibitors (§2.3.12).

**Radioprotector vs. radiomitigator vs. radiosensitizer — taxonomy.** Radioprotectors are given before exposure; radiomitigators are given after exposure but before symptoms manifest (relevant to accidental/occupational exposure response, distinct from the pre-planned clinical radiotherapy setting); radiosensitizers enhance tumour cell killing (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.11 §11.1–11.4). Amifostine (WR-2721)/its active metabolite WR-1065 mechanistic detail: beyond free-radical scavenging and DNA protection/repair induction, WR-1065 accumulates faster in normal tissue than tumour because normal cells have higher membrane-bound alkaline phosphatase (needed to convert WR-2721 to active WR-1065), while many tumours' lower vascular supply and acidic microenvironment further reduce their uptake and dephosphorylation rate — a genuine differential mechanism, not just a general antioxidant effect (Ch.11 §11.1.1). FDA-approved for reducing radiotherapy-induced xerostomia in head and neck cancer; safety-limited by non-life-threatening but treatment-limiting side effects (nausea, vomiting, hypotension) (Ch.11 §11.1.1). Cyclic nitroxides (e.g. Tempol/TPL) are notable for a documented differential redox behaviour: in normal cells TPL acts as an antioxidant/protectant, while in cancer cells it is reduced to a hydroxylamine form that loses protective capacity and instead increases cellular ROS, radiosensitizing the tumour while sparing normal tissue with the same compound (Ch.11 §11.4.6.1) — though the source notes nitroxide radiosensitization effects have been reported inconsistently across studies.

**PARP inhibitors as radiosensitizers.** PARP (poly-ADP-ribose polymerase) detects single-strand breaks and is central to base excision repair (see [[concept_dna-damage-and-repair]]); PARP inhibitors (PARPi) block this repair route, and have gained clinical interest as radiosensitizers because they combine several properties a good radiosensitizer needs (enhance tumour-selective damage without proportionally increasing normal-tissue toxicity, potential interaction with tumour immunity and repopulation) (Ch.11 §11.4.6).

**Internal contamination — decorporation.** For radionuclides taken up internally (inhalation/ingestion/wound) rather than external-beam-style exposure, a distinct set of medical countermeasures applies (blocking agents, chelation, isotopic dilution, displacement, surgical excision, lung lavage) — see [[protocol_radionuclide-decorporation]].

**Bystander effects (related, distinct mechanism) — a source discrepancy worth flagging.** Non-irradiated cells near irradiated cells can show DNA damage and reduced survival, implicating gap-junction signalling or released damaging factors; the IAEA handbook (2010) describes the phenomenon as "scientifically contested (reproducibility issues)" but notes implications for low-dose risk assessment (§2.3.11, §3.9). Baatout (ed.), *Radiobiology Textbook* (2023, Ch.2 §2.8.3, Ch.3 §3.20.3), by contrast, treats radiation-induced bystander effect (RIBE) as a UNSCEAR-defined, mechanistically well-populated phenomenon (gap junctions, exosomes, miRNA, clastogenic factors, cell-free chromatin — see [[concept_non-targeted-and-low-dose-effects]]) — the newer source calls the *mechanism* "still in its infancy" but does not describe the underlying phenomenon itself as contested. This looks like a 13-year shift toward broader acceptance of the phenomenon rather than an active, ongoing dispute like the [[concept_radiation-carcinogenesis|LNT debate]], but both framings are given here rather than silently picking the more recent one.

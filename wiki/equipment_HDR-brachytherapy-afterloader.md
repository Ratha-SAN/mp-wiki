## Synthesis

**What this equipment is.** The high-dose-rate (HDR) remote afterloading system is the core brachytherapy delivery device specified in WHO/IAEA's *Technical specifications of radiotherapy equipment for cancer treatment* (2021), Sections 4.1–4.2 (MP Library/Technical specifications of radiotherapy equipment for cancer treatment.pdf). The report's scope is explicitly narrower than brachytherapy in general: it focuses on HDR brachytherapy using a sealed cobalt-60 (or, adaptably, iridium-192) source in a dedicated afterloader, for gynaecological cancer treatment with customized applicators — the modality prioritized because of its role in the WHO cervical cancer elimination initiative that motivated the overall publication (see [[standard_IAEA-medical-physics-ethics]] for the broader ethical/institutional context in which such priority-setting occurs). Other brachytherapy sealed-source configurations (skin, eye, prostate, breast, oesophagus, bronchus, sarcoma applications) are outside this report's scope.

**Workflow.** A single sealed radioactive source, attached to a guide wire, is electromechanically driven to a sequence of programmed positions ("dwell positions") within a patient-specific applicator, with the dwell time at each position optimized to shape the delivered dose distribution — rather than multiple discrete sources (as in older manual/low-dose-rate techniques). The full HDR sequence — applicator placement and imaging, treatment planning, and delivery — is typically completed in a single ~2-hour session, with two to four such fractions delivered over a course of cervix cancer treatment.

**Cobalt-60 vs. iridium-192 source choice.** The report frames this primarily as a logistics trade-off rather than a dosimetric one: cobalt-60's 5.27-year half-life requires source exchange only every ~3 years, versus iridium-192's 73.8-day half-life requiring exchange every three to four months — a substantial difference in ongoing procurement/logistics burden, particularly relevant in the LMIC settings this publication targets. Dosimetric differences between the two isotopes are described as minimal despite cobalt-60's higher photon energy (1.25 MeV average vs. 0.38 MeV for iridium-192) — though the higher energy does require thicker shielding barriers, a genuine facility-design cost trade-off against cobalt-60's exchange-frequency advantage.

**Afterloader specification.** At least 20 channels (the report notes gynaecological applications alone may need only 3, but specifies 20 on the assumption more complex non-gynaecological techniques may be adopted over the unit's lifetime), a miniaturized cobalt-60 source of activity 74 GBq ±10% on delivery, precise double drives with a dummy source for pre-exposure catheter-length/safety verification runs, and a control unit with networked TPS connectivity, password-tiered access, complete activity/user logging, and hardcopy output of the full treatment protocol after each delivery. An internal battery/UPS ensures safe source or dummy retraction during a power failure, backed by a manual crank/long-handle-forceps emergency retraction path and an emergency container rated for up to 81 GBq cobalt-60 (see [[equipment_radiation-safety]] for this container's role as safety equipment).

**Applicator inventory.** The specification centres on CT-MR-compatible gynaecological applicator sets: Fletcher-type, interstitial ring (Vienna-type, 60° ring angle), vaginal/rectal, and endometrial sets in multiple intrauterine-tube diameters (3.5 mm and 5.0 mm titanium), plus a reconstruction box for gynaecological applications. If 2D planning via a conventional simulator or C-arm fluoroscopic unit is used instead of 3D CT-based planning (see [[equipment_simulators-and-imaging]]), metallic (non-CT-MR-compatible) applicators are noted as an acceptable, more robust and durable alternative — a genuine cost/durability-vs-3D-planning-compatibility trade-off the report leaves to departmental choice.

**Radiation safety, security, and QA.** Housed in a concrete bunker (thickness per IAEA Safety Reports Series No. 47, considering cobalt-60's higher energy relative to a comparable LINAC room). Distinctively, because the source is a portable, dischargeable sealed source rather than a fixed unit, the report specifies security measures beyond a standard controlled-area designation: locking the afterloader when not in use, secure door access, and out-of-hours departmental security — see [[equipment_radiation-safety]] for the full brachytherapy-specific safety-equipment list (area monitor, emergency container, lead apron for C-arm operation) this generates. QA priorities specific to brachytherapy (distinct from EBRT QA): checking applicator/guide-tube integrity (these degrade with repeated reuse), verifying source-positioning reproducibility, and — flagged as a "critical component" — ensuring the correct applicator and guide tube combination is used for each individual treatment, since a mismatch here (unlike most EBRT setup errors) is not otherwise geometrically obvious to on-console staff.

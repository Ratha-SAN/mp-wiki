## Synthesis

**Lesion spectrum.** A dose of 1–2 Gy induces roughly >1000 base damages, ~1000 single-strand breaks (SSB), and ~40 double-strand breaks (DSB) per cell (raw/Radiation Biology- A Handbook for Teachers and Students.pdf, §2.3.1). DSBs are the critical lesion for cell killing: initial DSB yield correlates with radiosensitivity at low dose, and unrepaired/misrepaired DSB correlate with survival at higher doses. High-LET radiation produces more clustered, complex DSBs ("locally multiply damaged sites," within ~20 nm) that are harder to repair (§2.3.1, §3.9). Approximately 10⁵ ionization events occur per cell per Gy, yielding ~1000–3000 DNA-DNA/DNA-protein crosslinks, ~1000 damaged bases, 500–1000 SSB, and 25–50 DSB (§3.7). Note: Baatout (ed.), *Radiobiology Textbook* (2023, Ch.3 Table 3.1), citing a different literature review, gives per-Gy low-LET yields of ~10,000 base damages and ~30 DNA-DNA + ~150 DNA-protein crosslinks — DSB and SSB counts are consistent between the two sources, but base-damage and crosslink estimates differ by up to an order of magnitude. This reflects genuine variance across the DNA-damage-yield literature (different assay methods and assumptions), not a resolvable factual error in either source, so both figures are given here rather than one being silently preferred (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.3 §3.2.6).

**Repair pathways.**
- *DSB repair* has two primary routes: non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) — operates throughout the cycle but dominates G1, is error-prone, requires Ku70/Ku80, DNA-PKcs, XRCC4/Ligase IV — and homologous recombination (HR) — restricted to late S/G2 (requires a sister chromatid), uses NBS1/MRE11/RAD50, RAD51/BRCA2, is high-fidelity (§2.3.2, §3.5). γ-H2AX phosphorylation is an early, quantifiable marker of DSB formation and repair-focus recruitment (§3.9). NHEJ mechanistic detail: the Ku70/Ku80 heterodimer detects and slides onto DSB ends, recruiting DNA-PKcs to form a synaptic complex; end-processing enzymes (Artemis — needed for only ~10–20% of DSBs, those with slow repair kinetics; WRN helicase; PNKP; TDP1; polymerases μ/λ) trim incompatible termini before ligation by the XRCC4–DNA ligase IV–XLF complex (ligase IV knockout is embryonic-lethal in mice, with extensive neuronal apoptosis) (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.3 §3.3.4).
- *A third DSB repair route*, alternative end-joining (alt-NHEJ / MMEJ / "backup"-NHEJ), was more recently characterized: an extremely error-prone pathway using 3–16-nucleotide microhomologies near the break, active throughout the cycle but augmented in G2, operating in both NHEJ-proficient and NHEJ-deficient cells via MRE11/CtIP-mediated end resection and DNA ligase III (mechanistically overlapping with single-strand annealing, SSA, a RAD51-independent pathway requiring 5–29 bp of homology). A major source of the chromosomal translocations associated with misrepair (Ch.3 §3.3.4.3).
- *Chromatin architecture* is now understood as an additional layer of DSB repair regulation beyond the biochemical/genetic and epigenetic systems above: local chromatin state (euchromatin vs. heterochromatin, and finer domains such as RIDGE/anti-RIDGE regions) affects both radiation-induced damage susceptibility and repair-pathway choice (Ch.3 §3.4.1).
- *Base excision repair (BER)* removes oxidative/alkylation base damage (glycosylase-mediated); *nucleotide excision repair (NER)* removes bulky helix-distorting lesions via global-genome (GG-NER) and transcription-coupled (TCR-NER) subpathways; mismatch repair (MMR) corrects replication errors (§2.3.2, §3.5).
- Hereditary DNA-repair-deficiency syndromes with markedly increased radiosensitivity and cancer predisposition: ataxia telangiectasia (AT, ATM mutation), Nijmegen breakage syndrome (NBS, NBS1), xeroderma pigmentosum (XP, NER), Bloom's syndrome (BS), Fanconi anaemia (FA), and HNPCC (MMR deficiency) (§3.5).

**Damage recognition and signalling.** ATM binds DNA termini and phosphorylates CHK kinases, which phosphorylate p53; p53 stabilization induces p21, which inhibits cyclin E–CDK2 to arrest the G1/S transition (G1 checkpoint), preventing replication of damaged DNA (§2.3.3). See [[concept_cell-cycle-radiosensitivity]] for full checkpoint biology. p53-mutant tumours (most human cancers) lose the G1 checkpoint but retain a G2 block. ATM signalling also activates BRCA1, c-Abl, NBS1 and RAD51 to initiate repair, and can trigger apoptosis via BAX/BID in radiosensitive lineages (haematopoietic, endothelial, germ cells) (§2.3.3).

**Chromosome damage as a biodosimeter.** Unrepaired/misrepaired DSBs produce chromosome aberrations (dicentrics, rings, translocations) detectable by karyotyping, micronucleus assay, or FISH. Dicentric frequency in stimulated lymphocytes is the standard biological dosimetry method after radiation accidents (500 metaphases scored; 25 dicentrics/500 ≈ 0.3 Gy total-body dose; Poisson-distributed after homogeneous exposure, with deviations indicating inhomogeneous exposure) (§2.3.4, §2.6.1, §4.2.1). Dicentric frequency decays with a ~3-year half-life, limiting retrospective use; stable translocations via FISH are used for older exposures (§2.6.1). See [[concept_biodosimetry-techniques]] for the fuller methods survey (CBMN, FISH/M-FISH/mBAND, PCC assay, γH2AX foci, gene-expression signatures) and their respective sensitivity/timing tradeoffs.

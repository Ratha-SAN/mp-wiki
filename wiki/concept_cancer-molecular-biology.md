## Synthesis

**Oncogenes vs. tumour suppressor genes.** Mutation/deregulation of multiple genes (out of ~30,000, likely <10% relevant to carcinogenesis) in a single cell is required for malignant transformation (raw/Radiation Biology- A Handbook for Teachers and Students.pdf, §3.3.3). Oncogenes arise from gain-of-function mutation of a proto-oncogene in one allele and act dominantly (e.g. RAS, activated by point mutation trapping it GTP-bound/active) (§3.3.2.3, §3.3.3.1). Tumour suppressor genes require loss-of-function of both alleles (Knudson's "two-hit" hypothesis, established via familial vs. sporadic retinoblastoma) and act recessively at the cellular level; loss of heterozygosity (LOH) is the hallmark somatic event (§3.3.3.2). p53, mutated in >50% of human cancers, is the most prominent tumour suppressor: it triggers G1 arrest (via p21) and apoptosis in response to DNA damage/hypoxia/oncogene activation; germline heterozygous mutation causes Li-Fraumeni syndrome (§3.3.3.3; see [[concept_cell-cycle-radiosensitivity]] and [[concept_dna-damage-and-repair]] for its checkpoint role).

**Familial tumour-suppressor syndromes (beyond p53/Li-Fraumeni).** Consistent with the two-hit model above: BRCA1/BRCA2 (transcriptional regulation, DNA repair — familial breast/ovarian cancer; see [[concept_dna-damage-and-repair]] for their HR role), RB1 (cell-cycle transcriptional regulator — familial retinoblastoma, osteosarcoma), APC (β-catenin degradation — familial adenomatous polyposis), NF2 (cytoskeleton-membrane linkage — neurofibromatosis-predisposition syndrome), E-cadherin (cell adhesion — familial gastric cancer), p16 (Cdk inhibitor — familial melanoma) (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.3 §3.12.2). Gap junctions (connexin-based, Cx43 most studied, 21 human isoforms, 2–3 nm pore) provide a fast direct-contact route for exchanging ions/metabolites/second-messengers between cells and can redistribute after irradiation (studied via Cx43 trafficking in irradiated fibroblasts/endothelial/breast-cancer cells) — see [[concept_non-targeted-and-low-dose-effects]] for how this connects to bystander signalling (Ch.3 §3.13.1).

**Growth-factor signalling.** Receptor protein tyrosine kinases (e.g. EGFR, HER2/neu) activate downstream cascades including RAS→RAF→MEK→ERK (MAPK pathway) and PI3K→Akt/PKB (phosphoinositide pathway); dysregulation (receptor overexpression, activating RAS/BRAF mutation, PTEN loss) is common in cancer and is separately implicated in intrinsic radioresistance (§3.3.2, §3.9). EGFR/HER2 overexpression occurs in HNSCC, glioma, breast, lung, colorectal, and prostate cancers and correlates with poorer radiotherapy outcome; EGFR-targeted agents (cetuximab) show radiosensitizing clinical benefit in HNSCC (§3.3.2.1, §3.9, §3.14).

**Molecular techniques referenced in the source (context for interpreting older literature).** Southern/Northern/Western blotting, PCR/RT-PCR, DNA sequencing (dideoxy chain termination), microarray analysis, flow cytometry (cell-cycle/DNA-content analysis via propidium iodide, BrdU labelling, Ki-67), and proteomics (2D electrophoresis + mass spectrometry) (§3.3.1, §3.6.2). These underlie historical measurements of labeling index, Tpot, and cell-cycle distribution referenced in [[concept_tumour-radiobiology]].

**Genomic/proteomic radiation response.** Ionizing radiation induces early-response genes (EGR-1, GADD genes, p21) via radiation-responsive promoter elements (CArG motifs), and modifies signalling through MAPK/SAPK/p38 pathways; radiation-induced cytokine cascades (TGF-β, TNF-α, IL-1) contribute to tissue-level inflammation and fibrosis — see [[concept_normal-tissue-response]] (§3.9).

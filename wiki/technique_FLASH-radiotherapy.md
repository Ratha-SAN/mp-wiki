## Synthesis

**Definition.** FLASH radiotherapy delivers dose at ultra-high dose rate (>100 Gy/s, vs. ~0.1–0.4 Gy/s conventional), compressing delivery time from minutes to under ~200 ms, with the goal of sparing normal tissue while maintaining tumour control ("the FLASH effect") (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.6 §6.4.2.1). A Swiss expert group has proposed that FLASH studies should report number of pulses, instantaneous intra-pulse dose rate (≥10⁴ Gy/s), and total exposure time (<100 ms) (Ch.6 §6.4.2.3).

**Mechanistic hypotheses (competing, not yet resolved) — updates the brief note already in [[concept_RBE]].** Three hypotheses have been proposed, and the leading one has been challenged by more recent data:
- *Oxygen depletion hypothesis* — ultra-high dose rate rapidly consumes local oxygen, causing transient hypoxia and radioprotection (less peroxyl-radical-mediated degradation of nucleic acids/lipids) (Ch.6 §6.4.2.1). This was the original leading explanation, but has since been challenged: studies using solid optical oxygen sensors found FLASH RT does not significantly reduce tissue oxygen concentration compared to conventional RT (Ch.6 §6.4.2.1) — so the mechanism remains genuinely unresolved, not settled in favour of oxygen depletion as earlier framing (including this wiki's own [[concept_RBE]] entry) implied.
- *Differential ROS-damage recovery hypothesis* — normal cells have greater capacity than tumour cells to detoxify peroxidized compounds, which would explain why tumours respond similarly whether hypoxic or normoxic under FLASH (Ch.6 §6.4.2.1).
- *Immune hypothesis* — builds on the oxygen-depletion idea, proposing FLASH spares radiosensitive circulating/resident immune cells (as little as 0.5 Gy can reduce lymphocyte survival by 90% under conventional dose rates), preserving antitumour immune surveillance and potentially enabling synergy with immunotherapy (Ch.6 §6.4.2.3).

**Clinical status.** As of this source, FLASH RT is not yet in routine clinical use; the one reported human case (cutaneous lymphoma covering the entire skin surface, prior conventional RT tolerance exhausted) received 15 Gy in a single 90 ms electron pulse, achieving complete tumour ablation with no skin toxicity at 6-month follow-up (Ch.6 §6.4.2.2). Most FLASH research has used electrons; photon and proton FLASH remain preclinical. Candidate future indications: late-responding-tissue tumours (preclinical data show dramatically reduced pulmonary fibrosis and neurocognitive impairment with FLASH) and painful bone metastases (Ch.6 §6.4.2.2–6.4.2.4).

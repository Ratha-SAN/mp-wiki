## Synthesis

**Tumour growth.** Tumour growth is approximately exponential with a characteristic volume doubling time (TD), but the growth fraction is <1 (not all cells proliferate) and cell loss is substantial, so TD (typically 2–3 months for human tumours) is much longer than the underlying cell cycle time (Tc, 2–3 days) or potential doubling time (Tpot, 4–20 days) (raw/Radiation Biology- A Handbook for Teachers and Students.pdf, §2.4.1, §3.6.1). Tumours are rarely detected below ~1 g (~10⁹ cells, ~30 volume doublings from a single founder cell); most tumours arise from a single transformed cell (clonality demonstrated via X-inactivation mosaicism, chromosomal markers e.g. Philadelphia chromosome, immunoglobulin/TCR rearrangements) (§3.6.1).

**Tumour stem cells and control.** Tumour control requires killing all clonogenic ("stem") cells — cells with unlimited proliferative capacity — not merely inducing growth delay (§2.4.2, §3.10). Assuming Poisson-distributed random cell kill, the probability of zero surviving clonogens is P₀ = e^(-a), where a is the mean number of surviving cells predicted from the [[concept_cell-death-and-survival-curves|survival curve]]; this generates the sigmoid tumour-control-probability (TCP) curve (§2.4.3, §3.10). Larger tumours (more clonogens) require higher doses for the same control probability (§2.4.3). "Radioresponsive" (rapid regression) should be distinguished from "radiocurable" — rapid regression reflects high proliferative fraction expressing damage via mitotic death, not necessarily elimination of resistant stem cells (§2.4.2, §3.10).

**Cancer stem cells (CSCs).** A distinct, self-renewing subpopulation within a tumour proposed as a major driver of radioresistance and post-treatment recurrence, independent of and additional to the hypoxia/reoxygenation mechanism above: CSCs show high DNA-repair proficiency (via checkpoint proteins ATM/Chk1/Chk2/SMC1/TP53), low steady-state ROS with high ROS-scavenging capacity, slow proliferation (more time for repair), phenotypic plasticity (reversible conversion to non-CSC state via epithelial-mesenchymal transition), and upregulated anti-apoptotic/anti-autophagy signalling (BCL-2, survivin, Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, PI3K/AKT/mTOR) (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.3 §3.21.3). Targeting CSCs is proposed as a strategy to overcome radioresistance, complementing the reoxygenation/fractionation strategy already described here and in [[concept_fractionation]].

**Microenvironment.** Tumour response is modulated by hypoxia (see [[concept_oxygen-effect]]), extracellular matrix interactions affecting signalling (e.g. EGFR/MEK/ERK), and vascular damage/inflammatory cytokine induction, whose role in tumour (vs. normal tissue) response is less well characterized (§2.4.2, §3.10).

**Predicting tumour response.** Intrinsic radiosensitivity (SF2, survival at 2 Gy) varies widely between and within histological types (~0.1–0.9) and, because differences compound over ~30 fractions, small SF2 differences translate into large differences in cure probability (§2.4.5, §3.13.1). Clonogenic-assay-based prediction (e.g. cervix, head and neck) showed early promise (West et al., 1997) but poor reproducibility and limited clinical uptake (§2.4.5, §3.13.1) — the clonogenic assay's practical drawbacks are a <70% success rate for human tumour samples and a turnaround of up to 4 weeks, which motivated the search for faster alternatives (chromosome damage, DNA damage, apoptosis assays), though these show variable correlation against the clonogenic "gold standard" (raw/Basic Clinical Radiobiology.pdf, Joiner & van der Kogel, 5th ed., Ch.21). Potential doubling time (Tpot) was proposed as a predictor of repopulation risk during therapy but has not been validated (§3.13.1).

**Genomic/molecular predictive biomarkers — now in some clinical use, not just "unresolved."** Gene-expression-based predictors have progressed further than the IAEA handbook's "active but unresolved" framing suggests: a 10-gene radiosensitivity index (RSI, derived from SF2 measurements across the NCI-60 cell line panel via network analysis) is prognostic and predicts benefit from radiotherapy over surgery in breast cancer, and has been combined with the LQ model (see [[concept_cell-death-and-survival-curves]]) to derive a "genomic-adjusted radiation dose" (GARD), shown prognostic for radiotherapy outcome across several tumour types. High tumour MRE11 protein expression (see [[concept_dna-damage-and-repair]] for MRE11's DSB-repair role) predicts benefit from radiotherapy over cystectomy in muscle-invasive bladder cancer, replicated by an independent group. MGMT promoter methylation predicts benefit from adding temozolomide to radiotherapy in brain tumours — mature enough evidence that trials now test alternative chemotherapy specifically in MGMT-unmethylated patients. Breast-cancer gene-expression signatures developed for chemotherapy-benefit prediction (MammaPrint 70-gene, Oncotype DX 21-gene) are already in routine, cost-effective clinical use, though not yet validated specifically for radiotherapy-outcome prediction. HPV status (by in situ hybridization) is an increasingly routine head-and-neck-cancer biomarker, motivating de-escalation trials in HPV-positive disease (Ch.21). Caveat: DNA-PK-associated protein expression studies have produced contradictory good-outcome/poor-outcome findings across studies, illustrating why standardized discovery-plus-independent-validation-cohort methodology (as used for MRE11 and RSI above) is now considered essential rather than optional for this field (Ch.21).

**Clinical radiobiology by tumour type.** Fractionation sensitivity (∝ 1/(α/β)) is low for head and neck, lung, and cervix tumours; higher for breast; very high for prostate (α/β ≈ 1.5 Gy), motivating hypofractionated prostate regimens. Repopulation lag phase is several weeks for head and neck/lung, shorter for cervix, and negligible for prostate (§3.15; see [[concept_fractionation]]).

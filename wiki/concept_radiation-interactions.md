## Synthesis

**Ionizing radiation types.** Radiation is divided into directly ionizing (charged particulate radiation: α, β/electrons, protons, heavy ions) and indirectly ionizing (X/γ rays, neutrons), which deposit energy via secondary charged particles (raw/Radiation Biology- A Handbook for Teachers and Students.pdf, §2.2.2). Photons interact with matter through three dominant processes: photoelectric absorption (dominant <50 keV, scales with Z³), Compton scattering (dominant 100 keV–10 MeV, the therapeutic range, ~independent of Z), and pair production (>1.02 MeV) (§2.2.2.2–2.2.2.6). Attenuation follows I(x) = I₀e^(-μx); the half value layer (HVL) is the thickness reducing intensity by half (§2.2.2.7). Charged particles (protons, α particles, heavy ions) deposit energy directly along their track with a Bragg peak of maximal deposition near the end of range; neutrons are indirectly ionizing, transferring energy mainly via recoil protons in tissue (§2.2.3).

**Linear energy transfer (LET).** LET is the average energy deposited per unit track length (keV/μm), varying from ~0.2 keV/μm for Co-60 γ-rays to >100 keV/μm for α particles (§2.2.4, Table 2.2). LET determines radiation "quality" — see [[concept_RBE]], which rises with LET up to ~100 keV/μm before declining due to "overkill." Low-LET radiation deposits energy sparsely relative to DNA dimensions; high-LET radiation deposits densely, producing more complex, less repairable lesions (§2.2.4).

**Dose and units.** Absorbed dose (gray, Gy = 1 J/kg) is the standard quantity; exposure (C/kg air, historically Roentgen) applies only to air ionization; equivalent dose (sievert, Sv) weights absorbed dose by a radiation weighting factor to account for differing biological effectiveness; effective dose sums organ-weighted equivalent doses to estimate whole-body stochastic risk (§2.2.5). See [[standard_ICRP-radiation-protection]] for current (2007) weighting factor values.

**Dosimetry principles.** Detectors fall into three classes: those measuring absorbed energy directly, those measuring ionization (ion chambers — instant readout, good accuracy, poor spatial resolution), and those quantifying chemically induced free radicals (Fricke chemical dosimeter — linear to >150 Gy but unstable in air; thermoluminescent dosimeters/TLD, based on LiF, good spatial information but nonlinear above 10 Gy and oxygen-sensitive; film dosimetry for relative dose profiles) (§2.2.6). (UNVERIFIED — the source handbook treats this only briefly as teaching background; a dedicated `equipment_` page on ion chambers or TLD would need a protocol/manual-grade source such as TG-51 or an AAPM report, not yet in `raw/`.)

**Direct and indirect action, free radicals.** Roughly one-third of biological damage from low-LET (γ) radiation is direct action on macromolecules (mainly DNA); this fraction increases with LET (§2.2.7.1). Indirect action proceeds via water radiolysis: ionization of cellular water (≈80% of cell mass) produces the hydroxyl radical (•OH), hydrated electron (e⁻aq), and hydrogen atom (H•) within ~10⁻¹³ s, which then react with biomolecules (§2.2.7.2). The **oxygen effect** — molecular oxygen "fixing" radical damage to make it permanent — is the chemical basis of oxygen's radiosensitizing action; see [[concept_oxygen-effect]]. Radical scavengers (thiols, e.g. amifostine/WR-2721) can restitute damage by donating hydrogen atoms, forming the chemical basis of radioprotectors (§2.2.7.3, §2.3.13).

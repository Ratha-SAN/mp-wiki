## Synthesis

**What these documents are.** Four IAEA Postgraduate Educational Course (PGEC) lecture-note modules (Module VI.1-VI.4, draft 2, November 2013) that walk through the BSS's (GSR Part 3's) actual numbered Requirements for planned exposure situations in operative detail — generic requirements (VI.1, Requirements 6-16), occupational exposure (VI.2, Requirements 19-28), public exposure (VI.3, Requirements 29-33), and medical exposure (VI.4, Requirements 34-42). This is exactly the "Sections 2-5 operative Requirements text" that [[standard_IAEA-safety-standards-hierarchy]] flagged as unread from GSR Part 3 itself — these four modules substantially fill that gap for the planned-exposure-situation Requirements (Section 3 of GSR Part 3), though not for emergency/existing exposure situations (Sections 4-5) or the numeric Schedule III dose-limit tables.

**Module VI.1 — generic requirements (applying to all three exposure types).** Explains the regulatory-process hierarchy the BSS uses, in increasing order of control: excluded from scope entirely; generically exempted; requiring notification only; requiring formal authorisation (registration for lower-risk/more-predictable situations, or licensing for the most significant). Explains the notification-vs-registration-vs-licensing distinction (Requirement 7) and exemption/clearance (Requirement 8) in practical regulatory terms. Details registrant/licensee responsibilities (Requirement 9): organisational arrangements, documented delegation, valid safety assessments kept up to date, environmental impact assessment where relevant, likelihood/magnitude assessment of potential exposures, periodically reviewed operating procedures, accident/incident reporting arrangements, QA/QM, maintenance/testing/servicing, and safe radioactive waste management — responsibility that persists throughout the facility's lifetime and cannot be shifted by the act of being licensed. Walks through justification (Requirement 10, including specific BSS-deemed-not-justified categories: deliberate radioactive addition to food/cosmetics, frivolous use in toys/jewellery, human imaging for art/publicity), optimisation (Requirement 11, tied to dose constraints), dose limits (Requirement 12, government/regulatory-body responsibility to set and enforce, numeric values in Schedule III), safety assessment (Requirement 13, a process applied at multiple lifecycle stages — siting, design, construction, commissioning, operation, decommissioning — not a single document), verification of compliance via registrant/licensee-run monitoring separate from regulatory inspection (Requirement 14), accident prevention via defence-in-depth and engineering controls (Requirement 15), emergency preparedness scaled to facility complexity (part of Requirement 15/emergency planning), and formal investigation/feedback of operating experience (Requirement 16, with a defined structure: trigger event, independent investigator, prompt written investigation, documented lessons learned and corrective actions, verified closure, and appropriate archiving/dissemination).

**Module VI.2 — occupational exposure (Requirements 19-28).** Regulatory body responsibilities (Requirement 19: enforcing dose limits, approving optimisation/constraint proposals, requiring prompt reporting of overexposures) sit alongside employer/registrant/licensee responsibilities (Requirement 21, BSS §3.76's 12-point list: dose-limit control, dose constraints, optimisation including individual-dose consideration, policies/procedures, organisational arrangements, facilities/equipment/services, health surveillance, monitoring equipment, PPE, training, consultation arrangements, safety culture) and worker responsibilities (Requirement 22: cooperation, following procedures, proper use of dosimeters and PPE, reporting past dose history, no reckless acts) — importantly, exceeding a dose limit is a serious event requiring regulatory response (e.g. a pro-rata dose limit for the remainder of the year) but does **not** automatically mean suspension from radiation work. The radiation protection programme (Requirement 24) is built around **designation of controlled areas** (where specific protective measures are or could be required; delineated, signed per ISO radiation-warning-symbol convention, access-restricted, monitored, governed by local rules) and **supervised areas** (lower-risk, periodically reviewed but not access-restricted) — the worked example distinguishes a locked physical-barrier source store from an I-131 therapy room (where locking the door with a patient inside is unacceptable, so contamination-check signage substitutes) from a LINAC/HDR vault (where a light-curtain interlock substitutes for a physical barrier). Also covers the Radiation Protection Officer (RPO) role — distinct from, though sometimes held by, the "qualified expert" already defined in [[protocol_IAEA-clinical-training-curricula]]'s CQMP framework — and PPE/RPE selection and maintenance principles.

**Module VI.3 — public exposure (Requirements 29-33).** Government/regulatory-body responsibility (Requirement 29) to set dose constraints for public exposure (accounting for source/practice characteristics, good practice at similar facilities, dose contributions from other authorised practices, and interested-party views) and enforce Schedule III public dose limits — importantly, no single registrant/licensee is individually accountable for a member of the public's *total* exposure across multiple practices, since that's a regulatory-body-level aggregation problem, not an operator-level one. Covers registrant/licensee responsibilities (Requirement 30) including the waste-minimisation hierarchy (reduce generation → re-use → recycle → dispose, applied in that order), visitor protection (non-radiation-work visitors get public-dose-limit-level protection), and the discharge-assessment process required before authorisation of any practice giving rise to environmental discharges. Covers radioactive waste/discharge management (Requirement 31), monitoring/reporting (Requirement 32, distinguishing source monitoring from environmental monitoring), and consumer products (Requirement 33) — devices with deliberately incorporated or activation-produced radionuclides sold without ongoing regulatory control after sale, which test the justification principle directly (smoke detectors and tritium-illuminated watches are readily justified; antistatic brushes with Po-210/Am-241 are given as a harder case) and require the *provider*, not the regulator by default, to actively seek an exemption or authorisation determination rather than assuming exemption applies.

**Module VI.4 — medical exposure (Requirements 34-42) — the section of most direct relevance to this wiki.** Critically, **there is no legal dose limit for medical exposure of patients** — unlike occupational and public exposure, protection here relies entirely on justification and optimisation, directly matching the framework already stated in [[standard_ICRP-radiation-protection]] and SF-1's Principle 4 discussion in [[standard_IAEA-safety-standards-hierarchy]]. "Medical exposure" in BSS terms covers not just the patient but knowingly-exposed comforters/carers (e.g. a parent supporting a child during an X-ray, informed and consenting) and inadvertently-exposed bystanders (e.g. members of the public near an I-131 patient, who may not even know they were exposed — protection here relies entirely on decisions made before the patient leaves the facility). "Relevant parties" for medical exposure are enumerated identically to the CQMP team already documented in [[protocol_IAEA-clinical-training-curricula]]: registrants/licensees, radiological medical practitioners, referring medical practitioners, medical physicists, and medical radiation technologists. Justification (Requirement 37) operates at three levels per ICRP: generic procedure-level justification (by health authorities/professional bodies, comparing whether the same diagnostic/therapeutic outcome could be achieved without the exposure), screening-programme-level justification (population benefit vs. cost, including radiation detriment), and individual-patient-level justification (referring practitioner + radiological medical practitioner jointly considering appropriateness, urgency, availability of prior imaging, exposure characteristics, patient characteristics, applicable national guidelines, and pregnancy/breastfeeding status). Biomedical-research volunteer exposure requires Helsinki Declaration compliance, CIOMS/ICRP guideline adherence, ethics-committee authorisation, and predicted doses below agreed constraints. Optimisation (Requirement 38) covers equipment design features (fail-to-safety mechanisms, two independent beam-termination controls for high-energy/HDR sources, automatic timer/dose-integration cutoffs, secured override access, collimation/field-uniformity requirements), quality assurance (commissioning + at least annual QC testing + post-maintenance/post-software-change re-testing, performance/record audits, independent structured internal audits, scaled to facility complexity), calibration (by/under a qualified medical physicist, independently cross-checked), and **diagnostic reference levels (DRLs)** — explicitly diagnostic-exposure-only, not applicable to therapy, not treated as dose constraints, set as a statistically-derived range from professional-body practice surveys, with both upper-DRL exceedance (investigate for equipment/technique issues) and lower-DRL shortfall (investigate for possible image-quality compromise) triggering review. Pregnant/breastfeeding-patient protection (Requirement 39) requires signage at multiple points in the patient pathway plus verbal confirmation before exposure, with confirmed/unruled-out pregnancy triggering mandatory radiological-medical-practitioner review before proceeding. Patient release after radionuclide therapy (Requirement 40) requires a medical-physicist- or RPO-established dose assessment for comforters/carers/public contacts before discharge, written take-home precautions, and — a genuinely modern addition not present in older radiation-protection literature — awareness that gamma-emitting-radionuclide patients may trigger airport/border security radiation alarms post-discharge, warranting a facility-issued information card. Unintended/accidental medical exposure (Requirement 41) requires proactive minimisation (maintenance, clear responsibility lines, handover procedures for serviced equipment, specialist training, independent verification checks, override-access control) plus mandatory investigation of specific trigger events (wrong patient, wrong tissue/site, wrong radiopharmaceutical, dose/activity much greater or less than intended, inadvertent pregnant-patient exposure, equipment failure with significant-exposure potential) with a defined response sequence: notify practitioners, notify patient/representative, calculate/estimate actual dose, identify lessons learned, implement corrective actions, produce and (where required) submit a written report, and share with the wider professional/safety community subject to patient confidentiality.

**Historical note on source status.** All four modules are dated November 2013 and explicitly reference the **GSR Part 3 (Interim) 2011 edition**, not the final 2014 edition already ingested in [[standard_IAEA-safety-standards-hierarchy]] — Module VI.2's reference list even flags "the following references will shortly be replaced by DS453" (a draft-standard designator), confirming these are teaching materials from the transitional period. The Requirement numbering and substantive content match the final 2014 GSR Part 3 exactly where cross-checked (e.g. Requirement 12 dose limits, Requirement 37 justification), so no contradiction was found, but this wiki should treat these modules as a detailed teaching elaboration of, not a fully independent verification of, the final GSR Part 3 text.

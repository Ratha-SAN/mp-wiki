## Synthesis

**What this report is.** ICRU Report 67, *Absorbed-Dose Specification in Nuclear Medicine* (July 2002), addresses dose specification for internally administered radionuclides (diagnostic and therapeutic nuclear medicine) — a fundamentally different dosimetry problem from the external-beam and brachytherapy reports elsewhere in this wiki's ICRU series, since the radiation source is distributed inside the patient's own tissue rather than delivered from outside (MP Library/2002_@medphyslib_ICRU_Committee.pdf). The report's central message, stated explicitly in its Preface: unlike external-beam therapy where averaging dose over a target volume is a reporting convenience, in nuclear medicine the mean absorbed dose is often the *only* available information, and the report is candid that this mean can both overestimate and underestimate the true biological effect depending on how non-uniformly the radionuclide actually distributes.

**Why absorbed dose is fundamentally harder to interpret here than in external beam.** The report notes that even at a "clinically relevant" absorbed-dose level of 1 Gy, low-LET radiation traverses a 1 mg tissue mass with ~100 million particle tracks — enough for the mean-dose concept to be statistically meaningful even at fairly small scales. But nuclear medicine's non-uniformity problem is different in kind, not just degree: radioactivity itself distributes unevenly across four nested spatial scales simultaneously — organ, tissue element, cellular, and subcellular — and each scale can show a materially different picture (the report's own example: whole-body ¹¹¹In-antibody imaging in a human appears reasonably uniform across organs, while autoradiography of the same agent in a rat reveals sharply non-uniform cellular and subcellular distribution invisible to external imaging). Because clinical imaging resolution (~1000 cell diameters) cannot resolve activity distribution below the tissue-element level, cellular/subcellular non-uniformity must be inferred from biopsy autoradiography or microdosimetric modeling rather than measured directly in the patient being treated.

**Radionuclide decay type determines the spatial scale of non-uniformity's biological consequence.** The report organizes emission types by range in tissue, in decreasing order: photons (most penetrating, therefore the *most* dose-uniform despite non-uniform source activity, since photon range exceeds typical non-uniformity length scales); beta particles (β⁻/β⁺/electron capture, range from a fraction of a mm to several cm depending on energy — e.g. tritium's beta particles have subcellular range while ⁹⁰Y's reach centimeters); alpha particles (40–100 μm range in tissue, roughly a few cell diameters, with characteristic Bragg-peak-like increasing dose deposition toward the end of track); and Auger/Coster-Kronig electron cascades (nanometre-to-tens-of-μm range — genuinely subcellular). A single electron-capture or internal-conversion event can emit 20–30 Auger electrons in cascade for some radionuclides (e.g. ¹²⁵I, ¹⁹³ᵐPt), making these emitters intensely radiotoxic if incorporated directly into or immediately adjacent to DNA, but nearly inert if localized elsewhere in the cell — a striking illustration that for these emitters, subcellular targeting (not just organ/tissue targeting) determines clinical effect.

**Self-dose and cross-dose.** Absorbed dose to a target volume is decomposed into self-dose (from sources within that same volume) and cross-dose (from sources outside it but within the same organ/patient) — a distinction with no real counterpart in external-beam dosimetry, since external-beam sources are by definition entirely "outside." The target volume can be defined at any scale from whole organ down to a DNA segment; the report's own worked example is thyroid iodine dosimetry: ¹²⁵I's short-range (≤15 μm) Auger/conversion electrons cannot reach follicular cell nuclei from the colloid space where iodine concentrates, so cellular geometry alone determines whether ¹²⁵I is biologically effective, whereas ¹³¹I's ~450 μm beta range is long enough to irradiate the whole follicular cell population fairly uniformly regardless of the same colloid-concentrated distribution — the same macroscopic biodistribution, with opposite biological-uniformity consequences, purely because of the emitted-particle range difference. This same range-dependence is the direct mechanistic explanation for why HyTEC's short-range brachytherapy/Auger considerations (not the isotopes discussed there, but the same physical principle) and [[protocol_ICRU-56-beta-ray-dosimetry]]'s emphasis on matching detector/target depth to beta range both matter for essentially the same reason — particle range relative to the biological structure of interest determines whether averaging is a safe simplification or a dangerous one.

**The MIRD schema.** The Medical Internal Radiation Dose (MIRD) formalism remains, per this report, the standard and "robust" method for nuclear medicine dosimetry: mean absorbed dose to a target region per unit cumulated activity in a source region (the "S value") is pre-tabulated for numerous source-target organ pairs using anthropomorphic phantoms (the same mathematical/tomographic computational models discussed generally in [[protocol_ICRU-48-phantoms]]), radiation classified simply as non-penetrating (beta, absorbed fraction φ=1 if source=target region, 0 otherwise) or penetrating (photon, 0≤φ≤1) — a coarser two-category simplification than the multi-mechanism decay classification above, adequate for organ-level diagnostic dosimetry but explicitly flagged as inadequate for the more demanding requirements of therapeutic dosimetry. For situations where target mass itself changes during irradiation (fetal dosimetry as the radioactivity decays over a pregnancy, or tumor shrinkage/growth during targeted radionuclide therapy), the report gives a generalized time-dependent-mass formula integrating activity, absorbed fraction, and mass all as explicit functions of time — the S-value approach is explicitly stated not to be valid in these cases.

**Recommendation: patient-specific dosimetry for therapy.** The report's headline clinical recommendation is that patient-specific dosimetry (rather than the standardized population-average S-values used routinely for diagnostic dose estimation) should be performed whenever radioactivity is administered for therapeutic purposes — reflecting that therapeutic doses are high enough, and clinical stakes serious enough, that population-average anthropomorphic-phantom dosimetry is judged inadequate for individual patient dose optimization, in the same spirit as [[protocol_ICRU-48-phantoms]]'s conclusion that tomographic (patient-specific) rather than mathematical (population-average) models are required for individual-patient external-beam treatment planning.

**Biologically equal effective dose.** Because nuclear medicine dose rate varies continuously as the radionuclide decays and redistributes (unlike external beam's discrete fractions), and because RBE differs substantially by emission type (alpha and Auger emitters have markedly higher RBE than beta/photon emitters at equal absorbed dose), the report introduces the concept of biologically equal effective dose to allow comparison of a nuclear-medicine treatment against a reference external-beam fractionation schedule — the internal-emitter analogue of the BED/EQD2 formalism already covered in [[concept_fractionation]] and [[protocol_ICRU-89-cervix-brachytherapy]], adapted for continuously-varying rather than discretely-fractionated dose delivery.

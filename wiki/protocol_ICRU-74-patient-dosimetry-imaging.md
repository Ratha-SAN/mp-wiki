## Synthesis

**What this report is.** ICRU Report 74, *Patient Dosimetry for X Rays Used in Medical Imaging* (2005), is the first ICRU report specifically addressing patient dose from diagnostic and interventional radiology (radiography, fluoroscopy, CT, mammography) — distinct from every other report in this wiki's ICRU series, which address either therapeutic dose delivery or internal/external radiation-protection exposure (MP Library/2005_@medphyslib_ICRU_Patient_Dosimetry.pdf). Its explicit two purposes: to set and check standards of good imaging practice, and to assist in assessing patient detriment/harm — a dual mandate that recurs as a structural theme throughout the report, since the quantities useful for the first purpose (simple, reproducible, easy-to-measure beam-output metrics) are often not the quantities most directly tied to the second (organ-specific absorbed dose and stochastic/deterministic risk).

**Historical evolution of the field, and why it matters for reading older literature.** The report traces radiological patient dosimetry from the 1925 adoption of the röntgen (R) as an x-ray exposure unit, through 1950s–1970s interest in genetically significant dose (GSD) and active bone marrow dose (leukemia risk being the dominant early concern), to the 1960s introduction of exposure-area product (a beam-output metric proportional to both exposure and irradiated field area, more practically measurable than point exposure and more closely tied to whole-body integral dose and irradiated volume), to the 1980s SI-unit transition that replaced exposure with air kerma (AK, unit gray) and exposure-area product with kerma-area product (KAP, or equivalently dose-area product, DAP). This progression means that patient-dose figures reported in older literature (röntgens, rads, exposure-area product) require explicit unit conversion — not just relabeling — before comparison with modern kerma/dose-based figures, since exposure-to-absorbed-dose conversion depends on the medium (air vs. water vs. soft tissue) and can differ by up to ~60% between free-in-air and in-patient/in-phantom measurement due to backscatter.

**Deterministic vs. stochastic risk drives which quantity matters.** For most diagnostic radiology (low dose, single low-dose-rate exposure), stochastic risk (cancer induction) is the dominant concern, for which organ/tissue absorbed dose (or, cautiously, ICRP's effective dose for comparing techniques/protocols at a population level) is the relevant quantity. For high-dose interventional radiology (fluoroscopically-guided procedures with long exposure times), acute deterministic effects — skin erythema, moist desquamation, chronic radiation dermatitis — become the dominant concern instead, for which peak skin dose at the most heavily irradiated skin region (not organ-average or whole-body dose) is the relevant quantity. The report's own clinical photographs (acute dermatitis 3 weeks post-procedure, progressing to chronic dermatitis with dermal sclerosis and telangiectasia at 1 year, from a porto-caval shunt placement) are offered as a documented illustration of why interventional-radiology skin dosimetry specifically needs dedicated attention distinct from the organ-dose framework adequate for routine radiography — directly analogous to how [[protocol_ICRU-56-beta-ray-dosimetry]] treats skin as a dose-limiting organ requiring its own depth-specific dosimetric quantity, though for a different radiation modality and clinical context.

**Explicit caution against misusing effective dose.** The report is unusually direct that effective dose (ICRP's weighted-sum organ-dose quantity, developed primarily for occupational/public radiation-protection comparisons) should not be used to assess detriment/harm for an individual patient or specific patient group from medical imaging — because the ICRP's nominal risk (fatality) coefficients underlying effective dose were derived for a reference population that may differ systematically in age, sex, and health status from the actual patient population being imaged, and because effective dose assumes low-dose, low-dose-rate whole/partial-body exposure conditions that may not hold for interventional procedures. Effective dose remains useful for comparing different imaging techniques or protocols against each other, but not as a stand-in for actual measured or calculated individual patient risk — a distinction this wiki should observe wherever effective-dose figures are cited elsewhere (no other current page uses this quantity, but the caution is noted here for future reference).

**Application-specific quantities, and why plain air kerma isn't enough.** Beyond the basic dosimetric quantities (air kerma, absorbed dose), the report defines and recommends application-specific quantities tailored to how imaging beams are actually delivered: incident air kerma and entrance-surface air kerma (dose at the patient's skin, including backscatter), air kerma-area product (KAP, beam output integrated over field area — used because it is invariant to the distance between collimator and patient for a fixed beam, making it a robust output metric regardless of exact patient positioning), and CT-specific quantities (CT air-kerma index, weighted and normalized weighted CT air-kerma index, CT air kerma-length product) — flagged explicitly as provisional in this report, pending a dedicated follow-up CT-specific report (which became ICRU Report 87 — see [[protocol_ICRU-87-CT-dose-image-quality]]).

**Diagnostic Reference Levels (DRLs).** A practice-optimization tool distinct from individual patient risk assessment: DRLs are dose or dose-related quantity values (e.g. a percentile of a national/regional distribution of typical entrance doses or KAP values for a given examination type) used to flag departments whose typical practice deviates unusually far from the norm — a quality-assurance benchmark, not a dose limit or a directly risk-linked threshold. The report recommends incident air kerma/entrance-surface air kerma, air kerma-area product, and CT air kerma-length product as the quantities to use for establishing and applying DRLs.

**Monte Carlo as the practical method for organ dose.** Because organ/tissue absorbed dose usually cannot be measured directly in a real patient, the report concludes that Monte Carlo radiation-transport calculation — using the same computational-phantom methodology (mathematical vs. tomographic/voxel models) already surveyed generally in [[protocol_ICRU-48-phantoms]] — is the primary practical method for deriving organ-dose conversion coefficients from the easily-measured beam-output quantities above. The report notes reasonable agreement between different institutes' independently-calculated conversion coefficients for similar exposure conditions, but that dose-conversion coefficients are often specific to national/regional imaging protocols (beam quality, projection, patient positioning conventions vary by country) — a practical caution that a conversion coefficient from one country's literature should not be assumed transferable to another without checking the underlying exposure-condition match.

## Synthesis

**What this report is.** ICRU Report 44, *Tissue Substitutes in Radiation Dosimetry and Measurement* (1989), is a reference compilation of body-tissue elemental compositions and the phantom/detector materials ("tissue substitutes") used to simulate them, evaluated across 24 body tissues and 62 selected substitute formulations (MP Library/1989_@medphyslib_ICRU_Committee.pdf). It is the compositional/materials-physics counterpart to the dose-reporting protocols elsewhere in this wiki's ICRU series — the question it answers is not "how should dose be reported" but "what should a phantom or detector be made of, and how good does the match to real tissue need to be."

**Core terminology.** A *tissue substitute* is any material used to simulate a body tissue's radiation-interaction and/or dosimetric properties (the report deliberately avoids the older, often-misused term "tissue-equivalent material" for this reason). A *phantom* is a volume of tissue substitute used to simulate radiation interactions; phantoms are classed as homogeneous (single material), body phantoms (anthropomorphic, simulating shape/size/composition), or standard phantoms (≥30 cm cubic, used for cross-comparison under standardized conditions). *Bolus* is tissue substitute placed against the patient to add scatter/build-up or even out irregular contours. A *reference phantom* has precisely specified geometry, composition, and density — the report's own reference phantom is a 30-cm sphere of soft-tissue composition (10.1% H, 11.1% C, 2.6% N, 76.2% O by mass, density 1000 kg/m³).

**Selection accuracy requirements are specialty-dependent — not a single universal tolerance.** The report is explicit that how closely a substitute must match real tissue depends entirely on the application: radiotherapy dosimetry demands substitute-introduced uncertainty not exceeding 1% of absorbed dose (since clinical dosimetry as a whole targets <5% total uncertainty, and substitute mismatch should only consume a fraction of that budget); radiodiagnostic image-quality phantoms require linear attenuation coefficients within ±5% of the simulated tissue (tighter, ±1%, for CT and bone-mineral densitometry, since these techniques are sensitive to small compositional differences); radiation protection phantoms tolerate much looser matching (mismatch should not consume more than ¼ of the overall ±30–50% dosimetry uncertainty accepted at protection dose levels) — reflecting that these fields have fundamentally different accuracy requirements, not that some are being sloppy. This same logic — matching required accuracy to what the measurement is actually used for — is the compositional-materials analogue of the graduated Level 1/2/3 dose-reporting standard already seen in [[protocol_ICRU-50-photon-beam-therapy]] and [[protocol_ICRU-83-IMRT]].

**Recommended elemental compositions.** The report's Table 4.4 gives recommended H/C/N/O (plus trace-element) mass compositions and densities for 24 body tissues, superseding earlier, less-reliable compilations (e.g., ICRU 1964's simpler "wet tissue" approximation). Notable reference values used throughout radiotherapy physics: soft tissue (10.1% H, 11.1% C, 2.6% N, 76.2% O, density 1000 kg/m³) and skeletal muscle (10.2% H, 14.3% C, 3.4% N, 71.0% O + trace Na/P/S/Cl/K, density 1050 kg/m³) — these two are close but not identical, a distinction that matters for high-precision Monte Carlo dose calculation but is usually treated as negligible in clinical planning. Cortical bone (elemental composition dominated by Ca/P from hydroxyapatite, density 1920 kg/m³ in adults) sits at the opposite extreme of the body-tissue density range from lung (260 kg/m³ deflated, 1050 kg/m³ inflated-equivalent tissue density) — the two tissues most responsible for dose-calculation heterogeneity corrections in modern treatment planning.

**Biological variability is a genuine, quantified source of uncertainty, not just measurement noise.** Body-tissue composition varies meaningfully between individuals (e.g., adipose tissue water content ranges 10.9–21.0% by mass across cited literature, lipid content 62.0–91.0%) and by age (cortical bone composition changes substantially from infancy to adulthood, though inter-individual variation among healthy adults is small). The report explicitly cautions that these compositions should never be treated as fixed physical constants — they are population averages with a documented, non-trivial spread, which places a floor under how precisely any tissue substitute (however well-manufactured) can actually represent a given real patient.

**Practical substitute materials.** A150 plastic (a conductive, tissue-equivalent plastic) is identified as the most widely used muscle-equivalent solid for both phantom and detector-wall construction, matching muscle interaction coefficients and stopping powers within the ±5% tolerance across photon energies 100 keV–5 MeV, neutrons 10 keV–10 MeV, electrons 10 keV–2 MeV, and protons/alpha particles up to 100–500 MeV. Solid-water substitutes are validated to reproduce standard water-phantom depth doses within ±1% for cobalt-60 reference dosimetry — the practical basis for the "solid water" slabs used in routine linac calibration in place of a water tank. For soft-tissue simulation generally, the report notes that at higher photon energies (where Compton scattering dominates and mass attenuation depends mainly on electron density rather than atomic composition), phantom materials with mass density in the 900–1100 kg/m³ range give adequate soft-tissue simulation regardless of finer compositional detail — useful context for why many practical phantom materials (acrylic, polystyrene, water) perform similarly at megavoltage energies despite differing chemical composition, while diverging sharply at lower (kV, diagnostic-range) energies where photoelectric absorption is atomic-number-sensitive.

**Detector-material implications.** The same substitute-matching logic applies to radiation detector construction (ionization chamber walls, TLD material, tissue-equivalent proportional counters used in microdosimetry) as to phantoms — the report notes this connects directly to Bragg-Gray cavity theory and the W-value calibration constant already covered in [[protocol_ICRU-31-W-value]], since a detector's response depends on how well its wall/gas composition matches the surrounding medium's mass energy-absorption coefficient and stopping power.

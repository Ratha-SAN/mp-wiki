## Synthesis

**What this page covers, and how it relates to existing wiki pages.** IAEA *Diagnostic Radiology Physics: A Handbook for Teachers and Students* (2014), Chapters 1-3 (Fundamentals of Atomic and Nuclear Physics; Interactions of Radiation with Matter; Fundamentals of Dosimetry). This page captures the quantitative, diagnostic-energy-range (≲150 keV) depth these three chapters add beyond what's already in [[concept_radiation-interactions]] (a radiobiology-oriented qualitative treatment) and [[protocol_ICRU-60-quantities-and-units]] (formal quantity definitions without derivations or diagnostic-specific numbers) — this is a deepening/extension pass, not a duplicate. No contradictions were found between this source and either existing page.

**Characteristic X-rays and Auger electrons — diagnostic-energy specifics.** When an inner-shell vacancy is filled by an outer-shell electron, the energy difference is carried away either as a characteristic (fluorescent) X-ray or an Auger electron, with branching probability given by the fluorescence yield ω (K-shell ω rises steeply with Z: 0.007 for oxygen, 0.17 for calcium, 0.60 for selenium, 0.93 for gadolinium — Auger emission dominates at low Z, fluorescence dominates at high Z). For a tungsten target, Kα1/Kα2/Kβ1/Kβ2 characteristic X-ray energies are 59.3/58.0/67.2/69.1 keV respectively, computed as differences between shell binding energies (e.g. E(Kα1) = E(LIII) − E(K) = −10.2 − (−69.5) = 59.3 keV) — this is the mechanistic basis for the characteristic-line spikes seen in tungsten-target X-ray spectra, directly relevant to [[equipment_LINAC-and-cobalt60-teletherapy]]'s discussion of kV imaging/orthovoltage units and to any future mammography page (molybdenum-target spectra, where Kα/Kβ subshell splitting is often unresolved). Bremsstrahlung production efficiency scales as Z² and is under 1% even for tungsten at 100 keV electron energy — the low efficiency is why X-ray tube anodes run hot and require substantial heat-dissipation engineering (see forthcoming X-ray tube ingest, Ch.5).

**Photon interaction cross-sections in the diagnostic energy range (quantitative depth beyond [[concept_radiation-interactions]]).** Four interactions matter below 150 keV: photoelectric effect (cross-section τ ∝ Z^n/(hν)^m with n≈3.6-5.3, m≈2.5-3.5, both largest at low Z — a commonly cited simplified form is τ ∝ Z⁴/(hν)³), Thomson/coherent (Rayleigh) scattering (elastic, atomic-electron-collective, negligible energy loss — differential cross-section dσ_coh/dΩ = dσ_Th/dΩ · F²(x,Z) where F is the coherent form factor, x = sin(θ/2)/λ the momentum-transfer parameter), Compton (incoherent) scattering by free/weakly-bound electrons (the Klein-Nishina cross-section, reducing to the classical Thomson result as photon energy → 0; incoherent scattering by bound atomic electrons introduces an incoherent scattering function S(x,Z) that is zero forward and approaches Z at high momentum transfer), and pair/triplet production (threshold 1.022/2.044 MeV — essentially irrelevant at diagnostic energies, included only for completeness). K-edges are sharp discontinuities in the photoelectric cross-section at the K-shell binding energy (tungsten 69.5 keV, molybdenum 20.0 keV, copper 8.98 keV) — the cross-section jumps by roughly a factor of 5 just above the edge, and this edge structure is the physical basis for filter/contrast-agent design in mammography and for the choice of image-receptor K-edge materials (gadolinium oxysulphide, iodine) discussed in later chapters. For water, the photoelectric/Compton crossover (where the two contributions to total mass attenuation coefficient are equal) occurs around 30 keV; below this the steep Z³⁻⁴-dependent photoelectric effect dominates image contrast, above it the roughly Z-independent Compton effect dominates and contrast between soft tissues drops — directly explaining why diagnostic kVp selection is a contrast-vs-dose trade-off (elaborated in the forthcoming Ch.5-7 ingest).

**Attenuation and stopping-power formalism.** Linear attenuation coefficient μ = N_aσ (interaction centres per volume × cross-section); exponential (Beer's law) attenuation Φ = Φ₀e^(−μx) describes only *primary* (uninteracted) photons — scattered and fluorescent photons still contribute to fluence at depth, which is why Monte Carlo methods are used for anything beyond simple primary-beam calculations. Mass attenuation coefficient μ/ρ is used instead of μ for tabulation since it is density-independent. Mass energy transfer (μ_tr/ρ) and mass energy absorption (μ_en/ρ = (μ_tr/ρ)(1−g), g = fraction of transferred energy re-radiated via bremsstrahlung, ≈0 at diagnostic energies) coefficients quantify energy handed to secondary electrons versus energy that stays local. Electron mass stopping power splits into ionizational/collisional (Bethe-Bloch formula, dominant at diagnostic energies, roughly ∝1/β² below 100 keV) and radiative (∝Z²(T+m₀c²), the bremsstrahlung-producing term, negligible for water/tissue but non-negligible for tungsten even at 100 keV) components — restricted stopping power / linear energy transfer (LΔ, with cutoff Δ, conventionally 10 keV) excludes energy carried away by energetic δ-rays, matching the restricted-LET subtlety already flagged (but not resolved) in [[protocol_ICRU-60-quantities-and-units]]'s History section.

**Charged particle equilibrium (CPE) — new formal depth not previously in this wiki.** CPE exists in a volume when charged particles leaving equal those entering, in both number and energy — requires (a) a homogeneous medium in composition and density and (b) a homogeneous photon field over a region large compared to the electron range. Under true CPE, absorbed dose exactly equals collision kerma (D^CPE = K_col = Φhν(μ_en/ρ)); under the more realistic *transient* CPE (photon fluence attenuating with depth), D = βK_col with β slightly >1, though β≈1 is an adequate diagnostic-energy approximation. Because diagnostic-energy secondary electron ranges are tiny (sub-mm in water/bone even at 150 keV — Table 3.1: e.g. 100 keV electron range is 0.143 mm in water, 0.084 mm in compact bone), the dose buildup region is confined to the top of the skin, unlike megavoltage therapy beams where buildup extends 1-2 cm and produces clinically significant skin-sparing — this is a genuinely new, diagnostic-specific point of contrast with megavoltage-beam behaviour not previously documented in this wiki's therapy-focused pages (e.g. [[protocol_ICRU-50-photon-beam-therapy]]).

**Cavity theory — Bragg-Gray, Fano theorem, and practical ion-chamber dosimetry.** Bragg-Gray cavity theory (small cavity: dimensions ≪ charged-particle range, cavity doesn't perturb the surrounding charged-particle fluence, no interactions of *uncharged* particles within the cavity itself) relates dose in the medium to dose in the cavity via the ratio of mean mass collision stopping powers, weighted by the electron fluence spectrum: D_w/D_g = mean-S-bar_g^w. The Fano theorem (1954) — "in a medium of given composition exposed to a uniform field of primary radiation, the field of secondary radiation is also uniform and independent of the density of the medium" — relaxes the small-cavity-size requirement whenever cavity and medium have similar atomic composition, which is precisely why ionization chambers are typically built with air-equivalent or tissue-equivalent wall materials. For a large cavity in CPE, D_m/D_w reduces to a simple ratio of mass energy absorption coefficients; Burlin cavity theory interpolates between the small- and large-cavity limits via a parameter d (d→1 small cavity, d→0 large cavity). Practical ion-chamber dosimetry combines these: for a chamber with gas-equivalent wall, D_m = D_g(μ_en/ρ)_g^m, or in terms of measured charge Q and gas mass m_g, D_m = (Q/m_g)·W̄_g·(μ_en/ρ)_g^m — this is the formal derivation underlying the calibration-chain language already used informally in [[equipment_dosimetry-and-QC]] and [[protocol_ICRU-31-W-value]]'s W-value discussion, now given its full cavity-theory justification for the first time in this wiki.

## Synthesis

**Purpose and timing.** Decorporation therapy aims to reduce the internal radiation dose after inhalation, ingestion, or wound contamination with radionuclides (typically actinides — plutonium, americium — or fission products such as radiocesium, radiostrontium, radioiodine). Agents are most effective given immediately post-exposure, while the radionuclide is still circulating rather than already deposited in target organs (liver, bone are common deposition sites) (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.11 §11.3).

**Mechanisms, by category (Ch.11 §11.3.1–11.3.7):**
- *Blocking (organ-specific saturation)* — e.g. potassium iodide (KI, 50–100 mg oral, adult dose) saturates thyroid iodine uptake against radioiodine via three mechanisms: dilution of circulating radioiodine, saturation of the sodium-iodine symporter (NIS), and the Wolff-Chaikoff effect (transient inhibition of iodine organification, risking short-term hypothyroidism). Perchlorate is a viable alternative (1000 mg perchlorate ≈ 100 mg stable iodine in effectiveness), acting via NIS competition, and may have a better adverse-effect profile in vulnerable subpopulations.
- *Reduced absorption* — e.g. Prussian blue (FDA-approved, colloidal form most effective), a non-absorbable resin that binds ingested radiocesium/thallium in the gut, promoting faecal elimination (used extensively and successfully in the Goiânia ¹³⁷Cs accident). Silica-based sorbents have been tested for plutonium/americium/uranium/thorium capture.
- *Isotopic dilution* — increasing intake of water/milk/tea, or IV isotonic saline, dilutes and accelerates excretion of soluble radionuclides (e.g. tritium).
- *Displacement* — an element of different atomic number competes for the same physiological deposition site: calcium gluconate displaces radiostrontium from bone deposition; stable iodine displaces ⁹⁹mTc. Ammonium chloride (lowers blood pH) or sodium bicarbonate (raises blood pH) can be used to increase renal elimination of radiostrontium or uranium respectively.
- *Chelation* — organic/inorganic agents forming ring "chelate" complexes with metal ions, promoting renal/other excretion. DTPA (diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid, Ca or Zn form) has the widest range of use, particularly for plutonium; other chelators include dimercaptosuccinic acid, dimercaprol, deferoxamine. Chelator stability is affected by pH, chemical properties, selectivity, and competing-metal concentration.
- *Surgical excision* — for fixed contamination (e.g. embedded wound material); performed with radiation-protection/health-physics support using a wound probe; excised material is retained for radioanalysis.
- *Lung lavage* — invasive, general-anaesthesia-level procedure reserved for insoluble inhaled particulates (e.g. plutonium) deposited in the lung, following careful medical/dosimetric evaluation; performed via flexible bronchoscopy/bronchoalveolar lavage.

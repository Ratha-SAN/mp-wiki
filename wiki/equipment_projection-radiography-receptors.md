## Synthesis

**What this page covers.** IAEA *Diagnostic Radiology Physics: A Handbook for Teachers and Students* (2014), Chapter 7 (Receptors for Projection Radiography), read in full — general receptor physics, screen-film systems, and digital receptors (computed radiography and flat panel detectors), applying the DQE/MTF/NPS formalism from [[concept_medical-image-quality-theory]] to real detector hardware.

**General receptor properties — quantum detection efficiency, Swank factor, and DQE(0).** Quantum detection efficiency A_Q = 1−exp(−μ(E,Z)T) is the probability an incident X-ray interacts in the receptor at all; it is highest at low E, generally falls with energy except for a sharp jump at the receptor material's K-edge. Beyond simple absorption, receptor output also fluctuates because absorbed X-rays of identical energy don't all produce identical amounts of secondary signal (gain fluctuation, or Swank noise) — quantified by the Swank factor A_S = M₁²/(M₀M₂) from the moments of the pulse height spectrum, ranging 0.35-1.0 for practical screens (degraded from the ideal value of 1 by K-fluorescence escape, ~0.7-1.0, multiplied by optical-transport losses, ~0.5-1.0). Combined, DQE(0) = A_Q·A_S gives the zero-spatial-frequency detective quantum efficiency — the effective absorption efficiency once both interaction probability and signal-fluctuation noise are accounted for. The quantum accounting diagram (tracking the number of quanta/electrons surviving at each conversion stage) identifies the "secondary quantum sink" — the stage with fewest carriers, which sets the noise floor; well-designed commercial systems keep at least ~5 secondaries per primary X-ray at every stage, but screen-film (20 grains/absorbed X-ray) and especially CR (only 5 electrons reaching the photomultiplier) sit much closer to this limit than flat panel systems (~1000-2000 EHPs).

**Receptor blur mechanisms.** Beyond geometric unsharpness (focal spot penumbra) and motion blur, three receptor-intrinsic blur mechanisms apply to essentially all materials: oblique-incidence geometrical blurring (worst far from the central ray), the range of the photoelectron created by the primary interaction (~1 μm for 10 keV electrons, ~50-100 μm for 100 keV electrons — small but non-negligible), and reabsorption of K-fluorescence X-rays some distance from the original interaction site (a material is relatively transparent to its own K-fluorescence, since fluorescence energy sits just below the K-edge where attenuation drops sharply). Material-specific mechanisms add further blur: for photoconductors (direct conversion), lateral charge spreading during collection and trapping-layer effects; for phosphors (indirect conversion), light spreading within the scattering ("turbid") medium, strongly thickness-dependent.

**Screen-film systems.** The screen-film cassette pairs one or two phosphor screens (typically gadolinium oxysulphide, Gd₂O₂S:Tb, having replaced the less-efficient calcium tungstate CaWO₄ used before the 1970s) with silver-halide film, exploiting the phosphor's large quantum gain (~1800-2000 light photons per absorbed 50 keV X-ray) to compensate for film's inherent insensitivity to single photons. The photographic process requires multiple photons to strike an individual silver-halide grain within ~1 second to form a stable latent image (the physical basis of both film's long shelf-life against fogging and its non-linear Hurter-Driffield characteristic-curve response); development converts sensitized grains to opaque metallic silver with gain >10⁸, fixing removes undeveloped (still light-sensitive) grains, and washing/drying yields the permanent image. The H&D curve's toe-linear-shoulder shape, gamma (slope, typically 2-3), and latitude (usable exposure range) are all deliberately tunable via grain size/distribution and development chemistry, trading contrast against dynamic range. Reciprocity failure — the photographic grain's built-in insensitivity to low-light-level, extended-duration exposure (needed to avoid dark-fog buildup) — causes long exposures (2-5 s, as in tomography/mammography) to require 30-40% more exposure than short-exposure reciprocity-based extrapolation would predict. Screen-film's DQE is fundamentally limited by its ~20-silver-grains-per-absorbed-X-ray secondary quantum sink, producing an extremely rapid DQE rolloff with spatial frequency (essentially negligible above f≈2.5 mm⁻¹, even for "high resolution" screens with visibly non-zero MTF out to 10 mm⁻¹) — a key historical argument (settled by NPS measurement, not intuition) for why film's very high MTF at high frequencies was in practice contributing little diagnostically-useful information in mammography.

**Digital sampling, aliasing, and the sinc aperture MTF.** For a square detector element (del) of dimension d, the aperture MTF is |sinc(πfd)|, with first zero at f=1/d; combined with the sampling-interval-driven Nyquist frequency f_N=1/2p (p = pixel pitch), signal content above f_N folds back ("aliases") into the sub-Nyquist spectrum in "accordion-like" fashion, corrupting both signal and noise. Since digital detectors sample at the minimum achievable p=d, f_N is fixed at 1/2d — half the aperture MTF's first zero — guaranteeing some degree of aliasing unless additional blurring (from the detection medium itself) band-limits the signal before sampling.

**Computed radiography (CR).** Uses a reusable photostimulable-phosphor imaging plate (typically barium fluorohalide, BaFBr:Eu), which stores a latent image as trapped electron-hole pairs rather than emitting light promptly like a conventional screen; a flying-spot red-laser scanner releases the trapped charge, producing blue photostimulated luminescence collected by a light guide into a photomultiplier tube. Critically, the *stimulating* (red) light's lateral spread — not the emitted (blue) light's spread, unlike conventional screens — is CR's dominant resolution-loss mechanism. CR's secondary quantum sink (~5-20 electrons reaching the PMT per absorbed X-ray) is comparable to or worse than screen-film, giving CR a DQE roughly matching screen-film despite CR's digital convenience — CR fully replaced screen-film as a direct drop-in cassette-based upgrade path but has since been superseded in image quality (though not eliminated in the reusable-cassette niche) by direct digital radiography (DR).

**Flat panel detectors — the active matrix array.** The enabling technology is the thin-film-transistor (TFT) active matrix array, adapted from laptop-display manufacturing: hydrogenated amorphous silicon deposited on large-area (multi-metre) glass substrates, with each pixel row sharing a gate control line and each column sharing a readout data line — reading out one row at a time, distinct from CCD charge-transfer readout. Direct conversion flat panels evaporate a photoconductor (practically, only amorphous selenium, a-Se, is currently viable; HgI₂, CdTe, PbO remain proposed) directly onto the array; an applied field sweeps electron-hole pairs to storage capacitors with essentially no lateral spreading, giving intrinsic spatial resolution close to the ideal sinc-aperture-function limit and DQE that is nearly frequency-independent (though DQE(0) itself is capped by a-Se's modest ~8000-theoretical/1000-3000-practical EHP conversion gain, limited by geminate recombination). Indirect conversion flat panels couple a structured scintillator (typically needle-like columnar CsI:Tl, exploiting internal-reflection light-guiding along ~750 μm pillars, achieving roughly double the resolution of an equivalent-thickness powder phosphor) to a photodiode array; higher conversion gain (~1000-2000 EHPs) gives better DQE(0) than direct conversion, but the phosphor's inherent light-spreading blur dominates the system MTF and causes DQE to drop off faster with spatial frequency than direct conversion. At matched 200 μm pixel/sampling pitch, typical DQE at Nyquist is ~60% for direct conversion versus ~10% for indirect — meaning noise aliasing is severe for direct-conversion systems and comparatively negligible for indirect. General radiography (40-150 keV) favours indirect conversion (phosphors absorb diagnostic-energy X-rays more efficiently than thin a-Se); mammography favours a-Se direct conversion (its low-Z composition is well matched to mammographic low-energy spectra, simultaneously improving both absorption and resolution).

**Non-active-matrix digital alternatives.** Optically-coupled systems (lens- or fibre-optic-taper-coupled CCD/video camera imaging a conventional phosphor screen) suffer severe light-collection losses — best-case lens coupling caps near 20% even at 1:1 magnification, dropping to ~0.05% at typical 20× demagnification, creating a serious secondary quantum sink (as low as ~1 photon per X-ray) that direct-contact flat panel coupling largely eliminates. Photon-counting/energy-discriminating receptors (interacting with and reporting individual photons, or photon energy bins) can in principle eliminate amplifier-noise contributions entirely and enable per-photon energy weighting (improving SNR by up to ~2×), but per-pixel circuit complexity (~1000-100,000× that of a conventional pixel) has to date restricted the approach mainly to mammography and specialized scanning systems. Scanning geometries (pencil beam, slit/fan beam, slot/multi-line beam) trade extreme scatter-rejection efficiency and lower tube heat load per unit imaged area against far longer total exposure time and mechanical complexity — area-receptor (2-D matrix) imaging is preferred whenever feasible, with scanning reserved for applications where scatter control is paramount (dual-energy bone densitometry) or where photon-counting's per-pixel circuit cost is otherwise prohibitive.

**Digital image artefacts and the case for digital over analogue.** Moiré fringing (spatial interference between a stationary antiscatter grid's periodic structure and the receptor's own pixel periodicity) is avoided by oscillating the grid during exposure (a Potter-Bucky mechanism), same as for screen-film. Ghosting (gain changes carried over from a prior large exposure) and lag (residual signal persisting into subsequent frames) are particular concerns in mixed-mode (radiographic+fluoroscopic) flat panel systems. Digital systems' advantages over screen-film span image quality/dose (generally lower dose, greater dynamic range), workflow (no cassette handling, immediate image review, PACS transmission/archiving/search — see forthcoming Ch.16-17 ingest), and enabling capability (tomosynthesis, cone-beam CT, dual-energy imaging, computer-aided detection) — though the handbook is explicit that even flat panel technology has not yet achieved fully quantum-noise-limited performance at reasonable cost across all applications.

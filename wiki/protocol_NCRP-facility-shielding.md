## Synthesis

**What these reports are.** NCRP Report No. 147, *Structural Shielding Design for Medical X-Ray Imaging Facilities* (2004/2005) and NCRP Report No. 151, *Structural Shielding Design and Evaluation for Megavoltage X- and Gamma-Ray Radiotherapy Facilities* (2005), are the two facility-shielding-design reports that split and superseded the older NCRP Report No. 49 (1976) — 147 for diagnostic/interventional imaging (kV-range, no significant neutron production), 151 for megavoltage radiotherapy (MV-range, neutron production above ~10 MV accelerating potential). Both were already cited by name (but not previously ingested) throughout this wiki's equipment pages — e.g. [[equipment_LINAC-and-cobalt60-teletherapy]]'s "refer to NCRP Report No. 151" and [[equipment_simulators-and-imaging]]'s implicit shielding assumptions — this page fills that citation gap directly.

**Shared quantitative framework — shielding design goals.** Both reports use numerically identical weekly shielding design goals: **0.1 mSv (or mGy) per week for controlled areas** (annualizing to 5 mSv/yr) and **0.02 mSv (or mGy) per week for uncontrolled areas** (annualizing to 1 mSv/yr) — derived, in both reports, from the same underlying logic: controlled-area workers are assumed to spend their full working time in the area (occupancy factor T=1) and the design goal is set at half of a fraction of NCRP's own 10 mSv/yr-per-age-in-years cumulative occupational limit, specifically chosen so that a pregnant radiation worker's declared-pregnancy monthly equivalent-dose limit (0.5 mSv) is not exceeded even with continued area access; uncontrolled-area design goals are set to keep any individual (patient, visitor, non-radiation staff) below the 1 mSv/yr public limit. NCRP 147 uses air kerma (K, in mGy) as its design quantity since diagnostic-energy x-rays have negligible neutron component and wR=1 makes Gy and Sv numerically interchangeable at these energies; NCRP 151 uses dose equivalent (H, in mSv) explicitly because megavoltage facilities above ~10 MV produce neutrons via (γ,n) reactions in the accelerator head (see [[equipment_LINAC-and-cobalt60-teletherapy]]), requiring the quality-factor-weighted quantity to combine photon and neutron contributions. This is a real methodological difference driven by physics, not an inconsistency between the two reports — both design goals reduce to the same numeric value where wR=1 applies.

**A note on the underlying dose-limit basis.** Both reports' controlled-area design goal derivation cites NCRP's own **50 mSv/yr occupational effective-dose limit with a 10 mSv × age(years) cumulative cap** (NCRP, 1993) — the US NCRP framework, not the ICRP 20 mSv/yr-averaged-over-5-years figure already documented in [[standard_ICRP-recommendations-history]]. This is a genuine, source-documented divergence between the two national/international bodies' occupational dose-limit philosophy: NCRP's 50 mSv/yr with age-based cumulative control versus ICRP's 20 mSv/yr averaged over a fixed 5-year window (both derived from broadly similar underlying detriment analyses, per [[standard_ICRP-recommendations-history]]'s discussion of ICRP 60's own derivation) — noted here rather than silently resolved, since a US-trained physicist using these NCRP reports and an IAEA/ICRP-trained physicist using [[protocol_ICRU-50-photon-beam-therapy]]'s European/international lineage are working from two different (though historically related) primary occupational limits.

**Common structural framework — primary/secondary barriers, workload, use factor, occupancy factor.** Both reports share the same conceptual architecture, differing mainly in numeric input data: a **primary barrier** intercepts the useful (primary) beam and must attenuate it (plus any secondary radiation striking the same barrier) to the shielding design goal; a **secondary barrier** intercepts only leakage radiation (through the source housing) and patient/phantom-scattered radiation. **Workload (W)** is the time-integrated beam output at 1 m from the source (mA·min/week for kV imaging in NCRP 147; Gy/week absorbed dose at isocenter for MV therapy in NCRP 151) — NCRP 147 innovates by using a full kVp-weighted workload *distribution* (Table 4.2, from a 1996 AAPM nationwide survey of 14 institutions/~2,500 patients) rather than a single conservative kVp spike, since barrier transmission depends exponentially on kVp while the workload magnitude itself matters only linearly — a kVp-distribution-aware calculation is therefore far more accurate than the older single-high-kVp assumption. **Use factor (U)** is the fraction of primary-beam workload directed at a given barrier (e.g. NCRP 147's Table 4.4: floor U=0.89, cross-table wall U=0.09, third wall U=0.02, chest-image-receptor wall U=1.00 for its own dedicated workload distribution). **Occupancy factor (T)** is the average fraction of time the maximally-exposed individual in an area is actually present while the beam is on (NCRP 147's Table 4.1: offices/labs/nurse's stations T=1, exam/treatment rooms T=1/2, corridors/lounges T=1/5, unattended waiting rooms/storage T=1/20, outdoor/transient areas T=1/40) — both reports stress T describes the single most-exposed individual's presence fraction, not the area's aggregate occupancy.

**NCRP 151-specific: neutron shielding, mazes, and door design.** Megavoltage facilities above ~10 MV require additional shielding methodology absent from NCRP 147 entirely: neutron dose equivalent at the maze door (calculated via Kersey's method or a modified Kersey's method, alongside separate photon dose-equivalent and neutron-capture-gamma-ray calculations, all summed to a total maze-door dose equivalent), and door design (lead-lined doors, or the more complex direct-shielded door with its own design problems around neutron-capture-gamma-ray production directly at the door itself). The two-source rule (when combining barrier contributions from multiple radiation components, always use the more penetrating component's TVL/HVL) is flagged as a deliberately conservative simplification, most conservative for dual-energy machines where the individual energies cannot physically be delivered simultaneously but are nonetheless combined as if they could be. NCRP 151 also addresses special modern-delivery considerations absent from the 147/older-49 framework entirely: IMRT (higher leakage-radiation workload relative to isocenter dose, due to beamlet accumulation — motivating workload efficiency factors and decoupled primary/secondary-beam workload treatment), total-body irradiation (extended-distance treatment geometry), tomotherapy, robotic-arm SRS units (e.g. CyberKnife-type, explicitly out of scope for the equipment-specification document already ingested in [[equipment_LINAC-and-cobalt60-teletherapy]] but covered here for shielding purposes), and dedicated cobalt-60/intraoperative units — plus skyshine, groundshine, and photoneutron-activation considerations (activation products, e.g. the short-lived aluminium-28 already noted in [[equipment_LINAC-and-cobalt60-teletherapy]], and ozone production from air ionization in the treatment vault) that have no counterpart at diagnostic imaging energies.

**NCRP 147-specific: modality-specific shielding and the shielding-design-goal-vs-air-kerma-limit distinction for film.** Covers radiographic, fluoroscopic, interventional, dedicated chest, mammographic, CT, mobile, dental, bone-densitometry, and veterinary installations, each with its own workload distribution and use-factor data (Table 4.2/4.3). A distinct additional consideration unique to this report: air-kerma limits for radiographic film (§1.4.4) — protecting undeveloped film storage from fogging is a distinct design goal from protecting people, requiring separate (typically much lower) air-kerma limits at film storage locations, with no analogous concern in the megavoltage/no-film context of NCRP 151.

**Conservatism built into both methodologies.** Both reports are explicit about the safety margins baked into their recommended calculation methods (not just the design goals themselves): primary-beam patient/phantom attenuation is neglected (typically ≥30% real-world attenuation ignored); barrier calculations assume perpendicular beam incidence (non-perpendicular incidence always reduces actual transmission via slant-thickness and increased distance); leakage radiation is assumed at the maximum value permitted by the relevant IEC equipment standard, though real leakage is usually lower; and recommended occupancy factors for uncontrolled areas are described as conservatively high relative to realistic behavior. Both reports state their design goals apply only to new facility construction, not retroactively to existing facilities built to the superseded NCRP Report No. 49 standard, unless equipment or use changes trigger a new evaluation by a qualified expert.

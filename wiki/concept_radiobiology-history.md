## Synthesis

**Discovery era (1895–1908).** Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays in November 1895; Henri Becquerel discovered natural radioactivity in March 1896 (initially via phosphorescent, then non-phosphorescent, uranium salts); Pierre and Marie Curie isolated polonium and radium in 1898 and Marie Curie coined the term "radioactivity" (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.1 §1.2.1). Ernest Rutherford ("father of nuclear physics") established that radioactivity is spontaneous atomic disintegration, named α/β/γ radiation types, and developed the half-life concept; Paul Villard first observed γ-rays in 1900 (Ch.1 §1.2.1). Röntgen (1901), Becquerel/the Curies (1903), and Rutherford (1908, chemistry) all received Nobel Prizes for this work.

**Early radiation injury and the birth of radiation protection.** Toxicity (hair loss, radiodermatitis) was recognized within months of X-rays' discovery; early radiology pioneers (e.g. Guido Holzknecht) died from cumulative radiation exposure ("X-ray and Radium Martyrs"). The "radium girls" (dial-painters ingesting radium-based luminous paint, 1917–1924, New Jersey) and the 1932 death of industrialist Eben Byers from radium-water ("Radithor") consumption were pivotal events ending the era of unregulated "mild radium therapy" (Ch.1 §1.2.2). Formal radiation-protection organization followed: the British Roentgen Society's 1898 Committee of Inquiry, the 1925 First International Congress of Radiology (ancestor of ICRU), ICRP formed 1928, UNSCEAR and the US BEIR committee both formed 1955, and IAEA created 1957 (Ch.1 §1.2.2, Preface) — see [[standard_ICRP-radiation-protection]] for the modern framework these bodies produced.

**The "law" of Bergonié and Tribondeau (1906) — and why it's an oversimplification.** The often-cited claim that "cells are radiosensitive in proportion to their proliferative activity and inversely to their degree of differentiation" originated from 1906 rat testis experiments (Ch.1 §1.2.3). It has never been rigorously validated and has well-documented counterexamples: ataxia telangiectasia fibroblasts are hyper-radiosensitive despite a *lower* proliferation rate than normal fibroblasts (see [[concept_dna-damage-and-repair]]); SV40-transformed fibroblasts proliferate faster but are *less* radiosensitive than their untransformed counterparts; Li-Fraumeni syndrome (p53+/−) confers radioresistance despite genomic instability and cancer proneness (Ch.1 §1.2.3). A more defensible restatement offered by the source: "the faster cells proliferate, the faster cell death will appear [after irradiation]" — a statement about *timing* of manifest damage, not about the *magnitude* of radiosensitivity. Proliferation rate (Tpot) has not held up as a predictor of radiosensitivity or outcome (Ch.1 §1.2.3; see [[concept_tumour-radiobiology]] for Tpot's separate, also-limited role as a predictive assay).

**History of fractionation.** Claudius Regaud (Institut Curie) showed in 1911 that ram testes could be sterilized without severe scrotal burns if the dose was split into 3 fractions 15 days apart — the "French school" position favoring protracted, hyperfractionated treatment, opposed by the "German school" (Holzknecht, Wintz) favoring short, intensive high-dose treatment (Ch.1 §1.3.1). Henri Coutard's protracted fractionation (Institut Curie, 1920s–30s) successfully treated head-and-neck malignancies still difficult to treat today and established that fractionation over weeks, rather than single high doses, spares connective tissue. The now-standard 1.8–2 Gy/fraction, 5×/week schedule emerged from this empirical clinical experience rather than from first-principles theory (Ch.1 §1.3.1). Frank Ellis's 1967 nominal standard dose (NSD) formula was an early attempt to formalize isoeffect calculations; it worked reasonably for early-responding-tissue effects but "dramatically failed" to predict severe late effects at large fraction sizes, motivating the shift to the linear-quadratic model now used (see [[concept_fractionation]]) (Ch.1 §1.3.2).

**Therapeutic ratio — origin of the concept.** German radiologist Hermann Holthusen first formalized (1936) the idea of comparing tumour-control probability against normal-tissue-complication probability as a function of dose — the concept now called the "therapeutic ratio" or "therapeutic window" (Ch.1 §1.4; see [[concept_normal-tissue-response]] for the modern TCP/NTCP formalism).

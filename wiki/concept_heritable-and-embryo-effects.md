## Synthesis

**Heritable (genetic) effects.** No significant increase in heritable disease has been detected epidemiologically in ~70,000 children of A-bomb survivors (parental gonadal dose ~0.15 Gy average), nor in children of radiotherapy patients (raw/Radiation Biology- A Handbook for Teachers and Students.pdf, §2.6.7, §4.5). Because human genetic heterogeneity makes direct detection of low-dose mutagenesis in humans statistically infeasible, risk estimates rely on inbred-mouse mutation-rate experiments (notably the "seven-locus method") combined with human population genetics (§2.6.7, §4.5). Mouse data give a doubling dose (dose that doubles the spontaneous per-locus mutation rate, ~1 in 100,000) of approximately 1 Gy at low dose rate, assumed transferable to humans (§4.5). Risk is calculated as: Risk = Prevalence × (1/Doubling Dose) × Mutation Component × PRCF (potential recoverability correction factor, ~0.15–0.3, accounting for the fact that radiation-induced mutations are mostly large deletions, many incompatible with survival to birth) (§4.5). For a 1 Gy paternal gonadal exposure, estimated risk of dominant/sex-linked heritable disease in offspring is <0.1%; recessive-disease risk is essentially zero (§4.5). ICRP 2007 reduced its nominal heritable-effect risk coefficient 6–8 fold relative to 1990 (0.2%/Sv whole population, vs. 1.3%/Sv in 1990) based on this re-analysis — see [[standard_ICRP-radiation-protection]] (§4.7.1).

**Embryo and fetal effects.** The primary human evidence source remains the >1,500 Hiroshima/Nagasaki children exposed in utero (1945–46), followed 1948–1964 (§2.6.6, §4.6). Unlike mouse studies (which show dose- and stage-dependent gross malformations at doses well below 1 Gy), no dose-dependent increase in structural malformations was found in the human cohort. However, 18 children developed microcephaly and severe mental retardation; nearly all mothers were exposed during weeks 8–15 of pregnancy (the period of peak neural migration during corticogenesis), with a smaller effect for later-stage exposure (§2.6.6, §4.6). A dose threshold around 0.1 Gy is evident: no increase in retardation below this dose in either human data or mouse experiments; risk rises to ~40% at 1 Gy when exposure occurs in weeks 8–15 (§2.6.6, §4.6). Dose-dependent IQ and school-performance decrements were found for exposure in weeks 8–15 and 16–25 above 0.1 Gy, but not for exposure before week 8 or after week 25, even at doses >0.5 Gy (§2.6.6, §4.6). Pre-implantation-stage embryos show an "all-or-nothing" response — lethal damage causes early spontaneous abortion, while surviving embryos develop normally (§2.6.6, §4.6). Practical implication: after diagnostic radiology (e.g. abdominal CT) in pregnancy, uterine dose is almost always well below the 0.1 Gy threshold, and termination of pregnancy is very rarely indicated on radiation grounds alone (§4.6).

## Synthesis

**What this page covers.** IAEA *Diagnostic Radiology Physics: A Handbook for Teachers and Students* (2014), Chapter 5 (X Ray Production) — the diagnostic-radiology X-ray tube and generator, complementing this wiki's existing therapy-oriented equipment pages (e.g. [[equipment_LINAC-and-cobalt60-teletherapy]]) with the very different engineering constraints of kV-range, high-current, short-exposure X-ray production.

**Bremsstrahlung spectrum shape.** A thin-target electron beam produces a rectangular energy-fluence distribution up to the electron's kinetic energy; a thick target is modelled as a stack of thin layers, each contributing a progressively lower-energy rectangular distribution, superposing into a triangular "ideal" (unfiltered) spectrum — total energy fluence grows with the square of tube voltage, so X-ray tube output is roughly proportional to U_A² (a rule of thumb, ignoring characteristic-radiation and self-filtration effects). Real spectra deviate from this triangular ideal via self-filtration within the target (electrons stop within a range of a few tens of μm below the surface, so the beam is attenuated by the target material itself before exit) and via superimposed characteristic-radiation peaks once tube voltage exceeds the target's K-shell binding energy (69.5 keV for tungsten; for tungsten targets K-radiation contributes under 10% of total energy fluence even at 150 kV).

**Target/anode material choice.** Tungsten (Z=74) is the default choice for general radiography, selected for high bremsstrahlung yield (∝Z) combined with a high melting point and low vapour pressure (2757°C at 1.3×10⁻² Pa). X-ray production efficiency is nonetheless extremely low — as low as ~0.8% at 100 kV — meaning nearly all electrical input power becomes anode heat, which drives most of the tube's engineering complexity. Mammography commonly uses molybdenum (Z=42) or rhodium (Z=45) anodes instead, whose spectra are dominated by characteristic (not bremsstrahlung) radiation, giving better image-quality/dose optimization at the low kV (25-40 kV) used there — though digital mammography's wider dynamic range has made this advantage less decisive, and some manufacturers use tungsten anodes even for mammography.

**Focal spot geometry — the line focus principle and the heel effect.** The line focus principle angles the anode (typical anode angles 6-22°, commonly 10-16° for general purpose tubes) so the "effective" focal spot seen from the beam direction is smaller (by a factor sin θ) than the actual "electronic" focus area heated by the electron beam — this decouples focal spot size (image sharpness) from the heat-dissipation area (tube loading capacity), the two competing constraints. A consequence of the angled anode is the heel effect: X-rays generated deeper within the anode-side of the field suffer greater self-absorption than those from the cathode-side, producing measurably inhomogeneous beam intensity across the field (up to ~50% drop toward the anode side) — in projection radiography this is usually a minor, sometimes-unnoticed nonuniformity, but in mammography it is deliberately exploited: orienting the anode side toward the nipple (thinner tissue) intentionally matches the intensity falloff to the tissue-thickness falloff from chest wall to nipple. Off-focus (extrafocal) radiation from electrons backscattered off the anode and re-accelerated to land outside the nominal focal spot can contribute up to ~10% of primary intensity; because its effective focal spot is much larger, it has little sharpness impact but does increase patient dose and can expose anatomy outside the collimated field (e.g. the ears in skull radiographs).

**Anode thermal engineering.** Rotating anodes (a tungsten — or tungsten/rhenium-alloy, for improved crack resistance — disc spun by an induction-motor rotor, typically 3000-3600 rpm low speed or 9000-10000 rpm high speed) spread the heat load over the disc's circumferential focal track instead of a single stationary spot, enabling much higher instantaneous currents than stationary-anode tubes (which remain adequate only for low-power applications: dental, portable, or infrequent-exposure units). Anode heat capacity (energy stored at maximum permissible temperature, ranging ~200 kJ for general-purpose tubes to >1000 kJ for high-performance tubes) and maximum heat dissipation rate together define the tube-rating chart, which has four regimes over increasing exposure duration: (A) mechanical/thermal-stress-limited (sub-100 ms — cracks can develop from thermal gradients at the focal track), (B) maximum-permissible-focal-temperature-limited (up to a few seconds), (C) anode heat-capacity-limited (10s to 200+ s, relevant to CT and fluoroscopy), and (D) tube-housing maximum-temperature-limited (long/continuous exposures). Graphite heat sinks bonded to the back of the anode disc improve heat capacity (graphite's specific heat exceeds tungsten's or molybdenum's at high temperature) and dissipate heat via blackbody radiation; the newest "rotational envelope tube" design exposes the anode disc's back directly to cooling oil for substantially improved continuous-current capability, needed for CT.

**Generator waveform technology — a direct patient-dose and exposure-time trade-off.** Single-phase generators (1-pulse half-wave or 2-pulse full-wave rectification) produce 100% voltage ripple — since kerma output rises roughly with tube voltage squared, most of a half-wave cycle contributes little useful output, effectively doubling required exposure time and softening the spectrum (increasing patient dose for the same image receptor dose) relative to constant-voltage output. Three-phase generators (6-pulse, ~13.4% nominal ripple, in practice up to ~25% from transformer imbalances; or 12-pulse via combined delta/wye secondary windings, ~3.4% nominal, under 10% in practice) are markedly more efficient. High-frequency generators — the current state of the art — rectify/filter mains power to DC, then invert at tens of kHz before a final rectification stage, achieving ~13% ripple at low current dropping to ~4% at higher currents, with much smaller/lighter transformers than 50/60 Hz designs and fast (<250 μs) voltage/current control response enabling precise exposure timing; low-power units can house the entire generator inside the tube housing, eliminating high-voltage cabling. Capacitor discharge generators (useful where mains supply is inadequate) suffer voltage droop during exposure (~1 kV/mAs), complicating exposure of thick body parts. Constant-voltage (DC, near-zero-ripple) generators give the theoretical optimum for both dose and exposure time but have fallen out of favour owing to cost and complexity.

**Exposure control — falling load and automatic exposure control (AEC).** To minimize motion-blur risk, generators default to "falling load" operation: starting at the maximum permissible tube current for the chosen focus/voltage and progressively lowering current as the exposure proceeds (following the tube's rating curve), keeping the tube at maximum permissible power throughout rather than a fixed sub-maximal current for the whole exposure. AEC (mandatory except for anatomically-invariant exams like extremities) uses one to three radiation detectors (ionization chambers, positioned in front of the cassette for standard energies; solid-state detectors positioned behind the receptor for low-energy work like mammography/paediatrics, where a chamber in the beam path would itself show up as an artifact) to terminate exposure once a preset receptor dose is reached, with detector selection (e.g. lateral vs. central) chosen by the operator to match the anatomically dominant region of interest; a backup timer guards against AEC failure to terminate.

**Collimation and filtration — the dose/contrast/tube-loading triad.** Collimator light-field/X-ray-field congruence (achieved by positioning a mirror-reflected light bulb at the effective focal-spot position) is a specific quality-control check point, since field-edge penumbra can arise from either honest collimator geometry or from extrafocal radiation. Inherent filtration (anode, tube envelope, insulating oil, collimator window/mirror — typically 0.5-1 mm Al equivalent for the tube itself, plus ~1 mm Al equivalent from the collimator housing) is unavoidable; added filtration (aluminium or copper sheets, or K-edge rare-earth filters like erbium in some fluoroscopy units) is deliberately inserted to remove low-energy photons that would otherwise be fully absorbed by the patient without reaching the receptor — a minimum total filtration of 2.5 mm Al equivalent is required by regulation for general radiography. Added filtration increases the beam's half value layer (HVL, the standard quantity for beam-penetration/quality — thickness of Al needed to attenuate incident air kerma by half) and reduces patient entrance dose for a given receptor dose, but also reduces image contrast (since contrast is inherently higher for lower-energy, more differentially-absorbed photons) and increases required tube loading (mAs) to compensate for reduced output — an explicit three-way optimization the technologist/physicist must balance per examination type. Compensation (equalization) filters — e.g. wedge filters for lateral cervical-spine views, bowtie filters in CT — address a related but distinct problem: evening out detector-incident intensity across a field where body-part thickness (and hence attenuation) varies substantially, independent of the general dose/contrast/loading trade-off.

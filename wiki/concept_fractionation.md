## Synthesis

**The five Rs.** Fractionated radiotherapy response is classically explained by five biological factors (raw/Radiation Biology- A Handbook for Teachers and Students.pdf, §3.12, §3.17):
- **Repair** — sublethal damage repair between fractions (half-time typically 0.5–2 h; see [[concept_cell-death-and-survival-curves]]); allows a larger total dose to be tolerated when split into fractions given ≥6–8 h apart (§3.12.1).
- **Repopulation** — proliferation of surviving clonogens during treatment, increasing the cell number to be killed; matters most in early-responding tissues and rapidly proliferating tumours, becoming significant after ~3–4 weeks (accelerated repopulation), with local control estimated to fall ~0.5%/day of prolonged overall treatment time in head and neck cancer (§3.12.2).
- **Redistribution** — cell-cycle-phase-dependent sensitivity (see [[concept_cell-cycle-radiosensitivity]]) means surviving cells are non-randomly distributed; progression back into sensitive phases increases sensitivity to subsequent fractions (§3.12.3).
- **Reoxygenation** — hypoxic tumour cells regain oxygen access between fractions, increasing sensitivity (see [[concept_oxygen-effect]]) (§3.12.4).
- **Radiosensitivity** — intrinsic variation among normal and tumour cells (added as the fifth "R") (§3.12).

**Historical note.** The value of fractionation was established empirically well before the LQ model existed: Claudius Regaud showed in 1911 that fractionating a dose (e.g. 3 fractions 15 days apart) could sterilize tissue without the severe burns seen with single high doses, and Henri Coutard's protracted fractionation schedules at the Institut Curie (1920s–30s) established the clinical case for fractionation over the rival "German school" preference for short, intensive high-dose treatment. Frank Ellis's 1967 nominal standard dose (NSD) formula was an earlier attempt at an isoeffect calculation; it held up reasonably for early-responding tissues but failed badly at predicting late effects with large fraction sizes, motivating the shift to the LQ model described below ("raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf", Ch.1 §1.3; see [[concept_radiobiology-history]]).

**Isoeffect curves and the LQ model.** Different fractionation schedules producing equal biological effect define isoeffect curves (dose vs. fraction number/size/overall time) (§3.12.6). The linear-quadratic model extends to n equal fractions of size d: -ln(SF) = n(αd + βd²), giving the isoeffect relationship n₁d₁/n₂d₂ = (α/β + d₂)/(α/β + d₁) (§3.12.7). α/β (units: Gy) characterizes a tissue's fractionation sensitivity: late-responding normal tissues have low α/β (~2–4 Gy, steep isoeffect curve, high repair capacity, large shoulder); early-responding tissues and most tumours have high α/β (~8–12 Gy); some slow-growing tumours (e.g. prostate) have unusually low α/β (~1–3 Gy) (§3.12.7). The LQ model assumes complete inter-fraction repair and no explicit time dependence, which can cause serious underestimation of damage when interfraction intervals are too short or repair is slow (e.g. spinal cord) (§3.12.7).

**Altered fractionation.** Because late-responding tissues have lower α/β than tumours/early tissues, reducing fraction size (hyperfractionation, e.g. 2→1.5 Gy, more fractions, same overall time) can spare late toxicity while maintaining or improving tumour control — but requires ≥6–8 h between same-day fractions for adequate repair (§3.12.8). Accelerated fractionation (multiple fractions/day, shorter overall time) targets repopulation rather than repair sparing; CHART (continuous hyperfractionated accelerated radiotherapy, 3 fractions/day × 12 days) demonstrated the importance of repopulation as a cause of treatment failure, though a too-short (4 h) interfraction interval in one trial arm caused excess late toxicity from incomplete repair (§3.12.8).

**Biologically effective dose (BED) and NTD2.** The isoeffect relationship above is normally worked with in its standard named form, the biologically effective dose: BED = D(1 + d/(α/β)), where D is total dose and d is dose per fraction; two schedules are isoeffective when BED₁ = BED₂ (raw/IsoBED- a tool for automatic calculation of biologically equivalent fractionation schedules in radiotherapy using IMRT with a simultaneous integrated boost (SIB) technique.pdf). Solving BED₁ = BED₂ for a new fraction size d₂ given n fractions gives a quadratic with the closed-form root d₂ = [-α/β·n + √((α/β)²n² + 4n·α/β·BED₁)] / 2n, which is the basis of tools (e.g. "IsoBED") that derive a fraction schedule for one target volume that is biologically equivalent to a schedule prescribed for another — the underlying method behind [[technique_SIB-IMRT]]. Dose-volume histograms are commonly renormalized to NTD2, the nominal total dose delivered at a standard 2 Gy/fraction, to allow comparison of differently-fractionated plans against literature dose-volume constraints. A practical caveat: the LQ model's validity above ~18–20 Gy per fraction is contested (debated in the literature as the "universal survival curve" question) — relevant to hypofractionated/SBRT-type regimes, where BED-based comparisons should be treated cautiously. See [[technique_SBRT]] for the named alternative model (universal survival curve / standard effective dose, functionally equivalent to NTD2 above under a different name) and the additional non-LQ mechanisms (vascular damage, immune response) proposed to explain SBRT's clinical effectiveness beyond what LQ/BED alone would predict (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.6 §6.4.1.1). Note a genuine cross-source discrepancy on where exactly this breakdown occurs: "raw/Radiobiology Self-Assessment Guide.pdf" (Yu & Abazeed, eds., 2016, Ch.23, citing Song et al.) states the LQ model is considered applicable only below 10 Gy/fraction, not the ~18–20 Gy figure used elsewhere in this wiki — both figures are cited in the literature; this is flagged rather than silently resolved since it materially affects how cautiously BED/LQ comparisons should be treated in the SBRT dose range (10–20 Gy/fraction is exactly where the two sources disagree).

**Combined modality treatment.** Improvement in therapeutic ratio from combining radiation with drugs requires that toxicity to normal tissue not scale as much as damage to tumour; mechanisms include independent (non-overlapping) toxicity, spatial cooperation (local RT + systemic drug for micrometastases), normal-tissue protection, and enhancement of tumour response (§3.14). Isobologram analysis is used to distinguish additive, supra-additive (synergistic), and sub-additive (antagonistic) drug-radiation interactions (§3.14). Concurrent (vs. sequential) chemoradiation is generally more effective, partly by inhibiting tumour repopulation during the radiation course (§3.14).

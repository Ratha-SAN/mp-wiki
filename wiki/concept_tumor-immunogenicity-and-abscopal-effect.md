## Synthesis

**Overview.** Radiation-induced effects outside the treated field — abscopal effects — occur in both normal tissue (see [[concept_non-targeted-and-low-dose-effects]] for that mechanism) and tumours; this page covers the tumour-directed version, which is of active clinical interest as a route to systemic disease control from local treatment, particularly combined with immune checkpoint blockade (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.5 §5.12.1).

**Radiation-induced tumour immunogenicity.** Radiotherapy increases how visible a tumour is to the immune system via three overlapping mechanisms (Ch.5 §5.12.2): tumour antigens (protein-damage products, including from non-DNA radical/ROS damage, presented via MHC); tumour neoantigens (products of radiation-induced mutation/genomic instability); and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) — a family of signals released by damaged/dying tumour cells that recruit and activate antigen-presenting cells (macrophages, dendritic cells), which then present tumour antigen to T cells in draining lymph nodes. Documented DAMPs include cytosolic fragmented dsDNA/micronuclei, HMGB1 (released by dying cells, binds TLR4 on dendritic cells), extracellular nucleotides/ATP (via P2RY2/P2RX7, the latter required for tumour-antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell induction), calreticulin (surface-translocated, aids phagocytic uptake of dying tumour cells), and chemokines (CXCL9/10/16).

**The cGAS-STING pathway and dose/fractionation dependence.** Cytosolic double-stranded DNA fragments (from radiation-damaged nuclear or mitochondrial DNA) are recognized by cGAS, which activates STING, triggering type-I interferon signalling and Batf3-dependent dendritic-cell maturation required for cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell responses — mechanistically the central pathway linking local irradiation to a systemic antitumour immune response (Ch.5 §5.12.2.3). This pathway is dose- and fractionation-dependent in a biphasic way: more cytosolic dsDNA accumulates with increasing dose, but doses above ~12 Gy per fraction activate Trex1, an exonuclease that degrades the cytosolic dsDNA and blunts the immune response — meaning very large single fractions can be immunologically counterproductive despite killing more tumour cells directly. Regimens shown effective preclinically/clinically for triggering this pathway include 3×8 Gy and 5×6 Gy (Ch.5 §5.12.2.3). TGF-β, upregulated in irradiated tissue, is immunosuppressive (impairs dendritic-cell antigen presentation, blocks T-cell priming) and can abrogate the abscopal effect; TGF-β-neutralizing antibodies restore it in preclinical models, motivating combined TGF-β/PD-L1-targeting strategies alongside RT (Ch.5 §5.12.2.3).

**Clinical reproducibility.** Despite the mechanistic detail above, reliably reproducing a clinically meaningful abscopal effect remains difficult — the phenomenon is well documented in case reports (e.g. combining RT of a metastatic site with immune checkpoint blockade) but the underlying biology, and therefore how to engineer it reliably, is not yet fully understood (Ch.5 §5.12.1, §5.12.2.3).

## Synthesis

**What this document is.** IAEA Training Course Series No. 78, *Guidelines on Professional Ethics for Medical Physicists* (Vienna, 2023), is the IAEA's first dedicated professional-ethics guide for Clinically Qualified Medical Physicists (CQMPs), commissioned after a 2021 global IAEA survey (presented jointly with AAPM) found widespread lack of access to ethics training and understanding among practicing medical physicists (MP Library/Guidelines on Professional Ethics for Medical Physicists.pdf). It complements, rather than replaces, the CQMP code of ethics in IAEA Human Health Series No. 25 (based on older AAPM/UK publications) and explicitly builds on ICRP Publication 138's ethical-values framework for radiation protection — the same source already synthesized in [[concept_ethics-and-risk-communication-in-radiation-protection]].

**CQMPs as independent, ethically-accountable practitioners.** The document's starting premise: medical physicists work as part of a healthcare team but are professionally independent individual practitioners, meaning they exercise independent judgement not only on technical/scientific matters but on the ethical dynamics of their own decisions — they cannot delegate ethical responsibility to physicians or institutions. This independence is framed as the reason CQMPs specifically (not just healthcare workers generally) need explicit ethics training.

**Value frameworks, layered.** The classical four principles of biomedical ethics (Beauchamp & Childress): beneficence, non-maleficence, respect for autonomy, and justice. ICRP Publication 138 reframes these for radiation protection specifically as five core values (beneficence, non-maleficence, prudence, justice, dignity) plus three procedural values (accountability, transparency, **inclusiveness**) — this document adds inclusiveness as an explicit third procedural value, one not currently listed in [[concept_ethics-and-risk-communication-in-radiation-protection]]'s summary of the same ICRP 138 source (that page currently lists only transparency and accountability as procedural values) — noted here as a probable gap in that page rather than a contradiction, since both pages cite the same underlying ICRP document. A further proposed extension (Malone et al., cited but not adopted as consensus) adds honesty, solidarity, sustainability, and empathy.

**Seven guiding principles for medical physics practice, as defined here:**
- *Beneficence and non-maleficence* — promote patient well-being, avoid harm; the document flags that "good" and "bad" outcomes are themselves contested (e.g. prolonging life vs. quality of life), making transparent discussion of values among decision-makers necessary, not just accurate risk information.
- *Autonomy and dignity* — grounded in Kant's formula (treat persons always as an end, never merely as a means); requires fully informed, freely given consent for any intervention, and separately, patient consent before sharing information even with next of kin (absent a legal custodian relationship or an overriding public-health exception, e.g. dangerous infectious disease).
- *Justice* — governs allocation decisions under scarcity (the document's example: organ transplant waiting lists, or ICU beds after a mass-casualty event); patients must not be discriminated against by race, gender, or other social characteristics, and health is treated as a right independent of legal status.
- *Prudence and precaution* — new technologies/procedures/pharmaceuticals require adequately proven evidence (typically via clinical trials) before wide use; directly relevant to commissioning new radiotherapy equipment (see Scenario E below) and to how BED/EQD2-style dose-comparison caveats already flagged in [[concept_fractionation]] should be communicated to clinical teams.
- *Honesty and transparency* — a reciprocal duty (patients and staff owed truthful information both ways); includes patient rights to access medical records and institutional obligations toward transparent outcome reporting.
- *Accountability and liability* — healthcare professionals are not obligated to guarantee a result, but are liable for harm resulting from a documented lack of diligence relative to professional standards.
- *Inclusiveness* — equal access to healthcare opportunities/resources, with specific attention to populations vulnerable due to poverty, disability, minority status, or limited decisional autonomy.

**Workplace ethics.** Practice audits (peer review by independent CQMPs with no conflict of interest, comparable-or-senior qualifications, typically biennial) are identified as a core professional obligation specific to medical physics given its specialized, hard-to-externally-verify knowledge base. On medical errors: the document draws a sharp, practically important line — patients have a right to be informed of any error that reached them, but do *not* need to be informed of a "near miss" (an error caught and corrected before reaching the patient, e.g. a wrong treatment dose noticed before delivery) — however, near misses carry a separate, non-optional obligation to be reported internally (e.g. via an incident-reporting-and-learning system), since only aggregated near-miss data lets an institution prevent the error type recurring. The document explicitly frames most errors as system/process failures rather than individual fault ("errors are human"), and recommends redundancy (e.g. independent double-checks) and blame-resistant reporting cultures as the practical response. Whistle-blower protection and professional conflict-of-interest management (disclosure, recusal, or relationship termination — with the document noting that even a *suspected* conflict can be as damaging as an actual one) round out the workplace-ethics section.

**Scientific integrity and research ethics.** Plagiarism is defined per the FFP (fabrication, falsification, plagiarism) research-misconduct framework, extended to cover self-plagiarism and citation-padding as "unacceptable practices" even though not literally plagiarism. Human-subjects research requires ethics-committee (IRB) approval regardless of whether informed consent was independently obtained — consent alone is not sufficient authorization. Waivers of consent are permitted only under specific conditions: minimal participant risk, research value outweighing autonomy cost, practical infeasibility of obtaining active consent, and no reasonable indication that subjects would have refused if asked.

**Ten worked ethical-dilemma scenarios**, each stated as situation → recommended response → principles invoked, spanning diagnostic radiology (falsified/unperformed QA measurements; unreviewed repeat CT scans), nuclear medicine (falsified dose-calibrator linearity data; clinical-trial patient-selection criteria skewed toward insured patients), radiation therapy (administrative pressure to release a new linac for clinical use before commissioning is complete; a colleague offering a pre-filled, results-predetermined practice-audit checklist), radiation safety (a malfunctioning vault door interlock with a full waiting room; a vendor offering a paid contract to a Radiation Safety Officer contingent on selecting their product), and professional/contractual matters (a hospital delaying a required HDR brachytherapy source exchange while insisting treatments continue on schedule; a departing contractor CQMP withholding self-developed clinical software as claimed intellectual property). Common thread across the scenarios: professional refusal to participate in substandard or dishonest practice is presented as an ethically *required* response, not merely a permitted one, whenever resources or integrity are compromised — several scenarios explicitly endorse escalation to institutional management, regulators, or whistle-blower channels as the correct next step, not merely an acceptable one.

**Education framework.** The document recommends ethics be embedded in three stages: postgraduate academic curricula (as part of the IAEA's "Professional and Scientific Development" syllabus module, covering WMA ethical guidelines, clinical-trial basics, and research integrity/authorship), structured clinical residency training (assessed via portfolio review and observed handling of real conflicts of interest, supplemented by scenario role-play), and continuous professional development post-certification. Core recommended references: Beauchamp & Childress, *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* (2019); the World Medical Association's Medical Ethics resources; and applicable national ethics/conduct guidelines.

## Synthesis

**Age at exposure.** Radiosensitivity to carcinogenesis is highest in early life, decreases toward maturity, and rises again in old age (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.7 §7.6.1). "Attained age" (age-at-exposure + time-since-exposure) matters independently of age-at-exposure because baseline cancer incidence/mortality itself rises with age. A-bomb LSS data (see [[concept_radiation-carcinogenesis]]) show the sensitive cancer type varies by age-at-exposure: thyroid/stomach/solid cancer overall for those exposed young, breast cancer for those exposed at puberty, lung cancer for those exposed ≥40 (Ch.7 §7.6.1). Proposed mechanisms for childhood excess sensitivity: longer latency window before cancer manifests, faster radionuclide incorporation into growing bone, and a larger stem-cell pool capable of malignant transformation in growing tissue (Ch.7 §7.6.1). Proposed mechanisms for elderly excess sensitivity: age-related decline in DNA damage-response efficiency (delayed DDR kinetics, impaired NHEJ/HR recruitment — see [[concept_dna-damage-and-repair]]), telomere attrition and accelerated senescence (see [[concept_telomeres-and-senescence]]), increased baseline oxidative stress with compromised antioxidant capacity, and radiation-induced epigenetic alterations (Ch.7 §7.6.2). Clinically, children show far higher cumulative late-toxicity incidence after RT (>70% at 30 years post-treatment vs. ≤15% in adults at 3–5 years) — attributed to radiation affecting tissues still undergoing developmental proliferation (e.g. paediatric brain irradiation before age 5 causes more IQ decline than irradiation at older ages), whereas elderly patients' proliferative compartments are already shifting toward senescence, limiting self-repair after injury (Ch.7 §7.6.3). Three genetic syndromes (AT/ATM, LIG4 syndrome, Nijmegen breakage syndrome/NBS1 — see [[concept_dna-damage-and-repair]]) are specifically associated with fatal RT-induced non-cancer adverse events in children (Ch.7 §7.6.3).

**Biological sex.** A-bomb LSS data show excess relative risk (ERR) per Sv for solid cancers is roughly 2× higher in women than men (Table 7.7: all solid cancer ERR 0.38 males vs. 0.79 females; lung cancer 0.50 vs. 2.18, a 4.3× ratio) — this is a more granular, sex-disaggregated view of the same LSS cohort already summarized (without sex breakdown) in [[concept_radiation-carcinogenesis]] (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.7 §7.7.3, Table 7.7). UNSCEAR's conclusion: excess absolute risk (EAR) and ERR for total solid tumours run roughly 2× higher in women than men (varying by site/organ), with no sex difference for leukaemia (Ch.7 §7.7.3). Chernobyl data likewise show female-skewed thyroid cancer incidence (2.5× in contaminated Ukrainian territories) and increased spontaneous miscarriage of female fetuses. Mechanistic explanation is unresolved — proposed contributors include hormonal/immunological differences, epigenetic/genetic factors, and sex-chromosome gene-dosage effects (X chromosome: ~160 Mb, >1000 genes; Y chromosome: ~65 Mb, ~100 genes) — but animal gene-expression studies have not found a clear correlation between sex-specific gene expression and phenotype, so this remains an open question rather than a settled mechanism (Ch.7 §7.7.2, §7.7.4). **Important caveat directly relevant to radiation protection:** despite this epidemiological sex disparity, current ICRP annual occupational dose limits (see [[standard_ICRP-radiation-protection]]) do not differentiate by sex (Ch.7 §7.7.6) — flagged in the source itself as a research priority (MELODI, EURAMED initiatives) rather than a resolved policy gap.

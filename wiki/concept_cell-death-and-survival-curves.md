## Synthesis

**Radiobiological definition of cell death.** Cells are considered "killed" by loss of reproductive integrity (inability to sustain 5–6 divisions / form a ≥50-cell colony), not physical death (raw/Radiation Biology- A Handbook for Teachers and Students.pdf, §2.3.5). Modes of reproductive death: apoptosis (programmed, non-inflammatory; morphology = blebbing, chromatin condensation, nucleosome laddering), necrosis (inflammatory, high-dose), mitotic catastrophe (dominant mode for most solid tumour and normal cells, after failed mitosis), and senescence/terminal growth arrest (metabolically active but non-dividing) (§2.3.5, §3.7). Which mode predominates is cell-type dependent: lymphocytes, spermatogonia, thymocytes and salivary gland cells characteristically undergo rapid interphase apoptosis; most epithelial/solid tumour cells die by delayed mitotic catastrophe (§3.7).

**Survival curve models.** The clonogenic (colony-forming) assay is the gold standard, plating single-cell suspensions and scoring colonies of ≥50 cells after irradiation (§2.3.6, §3.8). The linear-quadratic (LQ) model is the standard clinical model:

  SF = exp[-(αD + βD²)]

where α represents single-track (linear) lethal lesions and β represents two-track (quadratic) interacting lesions; α/β is the dose at which linear and quadratic components contribute equally (§2.3.6, §3.8, §3.12.7). An older target-theory model, SF = 1-(1-e^(-D/D0))ⁿ, fits high-dose data but poorly describes the low-dose shoulder. Survival at 2 Gy (SF2) is used clinically because 2 Gy is a common fraction size (§2.3.6). See [[concept_fractionation]] for α/β values by tissue and use in isoeffect calculations.

**Hit-and-target theory (historical basis of the shoulder concept).** The predecessor to the LQ model treats radiation damage as discrete "hits" landing in a Poisson-distributed manner within a sensitive molecular "target"; D37 (dose giving on average one hit per target, SF = e⁻¹ ≈ 37%) and D0 (dose reducing survival to 37% along the high-dose linear region of a multi-target curve) are its characteristic parameters, and — notably — the model permits back-calculating an inactivated molecule's approximate molecular mass from D37 (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.4 §4.5.1). The single-hit single-target model gives a purely exponential (no-shoulder) survival curve; the multi-target single-hit model reproduces the characteristic shoulder-then-straight-line shape and was the standard fit used for decades before the LQ model (Ch.4 §4.5.1.3). Under this formalism, "sublethal damage" is damage confined to the target that alone is insufficient to inactivate — conceptually the ancestor of the LQ model's SSB/DSB sublethal-damage framework, though the two models are not derived the same way (Ch.4 §4.5.1.4, §4.5.6.1).

**Mechanistic basis for the LQ model (ATM shuttling).** Bodgi & Foray (2016) proposed a mechanistic derivation of the LQ model based on nucleo-shuttling of radiation-induced ATM monomers: DSB recognition depends on ATM monomers generated in the cytoplasm diffusing into the nucleus, with DSB-production and monomerization rates assumed linear-quadratic in dose — providing a molecular rationale for the LQ model's α and β terms rather than treating them as purely empirical fit parameters (Ch.4 §4.5.4.1).

**Modifications to the LQ model at dose extremes.** At doses <0.6–1 Gy, "low-dose hypersensitivity" is observed: survival is lower than the LQ extrapolation predicts (this is the same low-dose hypersensitivity phenomenon as HRS — see [[concept_non-targeted-and-low-dose-effects]]); Joiner & Johns proposed making the α parameter itself dose-dependent (decaying from an elevated low-dose value) to fit this region (Ch.4 §4.5.4.1). At high doses (relevant to hypofractionation/SRS), the basic LQ model underestimates survival and fails to reproduce the further straightening of the curve seen experimentally; a "linear-quadratic-linear" (LQL) modification adds a repair-rate-dependent term that converges to linear behaviour at high acute doses (Ch.4 §4.5.6). Both modifications fit their respective dose regions well but introduce additional parameters requiring independent experimental determination (Ch.4 §4.5.4.1, §4.5.6).

**Potentially lethal damage repair — classic evidence.** Elkind & Sutton (1959) demonstrated with V79 Chinese hamster cells that splitting a dose into two fractions 18 h apart substantially raised survival relative to an equivalent single acute dose — the founding experiment behind sublethal damage repair (SLDR) described above (Ch.4 §4.5.6.1). Potentially lethal damage repair (PLDR, already noted above) has a specific chromosomal mechanism: if a cell carries an unrepaired DSB into S phase, it can form an asymmetric (dicentric/ring) chromosomal aberration that is lethal upon mitosis; giving the cell time to repair the DSB before S-phase entry prevents this, which is why suboptimal growth conditions or cell-cycle inhibitors (which delay S-phase entry) increase measured survival (Ch.4 §4.5.6.1).

**Repair kinetics visible in survival curves.** Sublethal damage repair (SLDR, "Elkind repair") raises survival when a dose is split with a gap of hours; half-time of repair is typically 0.5–2 h in most tissues (longer in CNS, ~4+ h) (§2.3.9, §3.9, §3.12.1). Potentially lethal damage repair (PLDR) is repair that occurs when cells are held in suboptimal growth conditions (plateau phase, low temperature) after irradiation before replating (§2.3.9, §3.9). Repair is negligible at high dose rates (1–5 Gy/min, external beam/HDR brachytherapy) but substantial at low dose rates (LDR brachytherapy, 1.6–150 cGy/min) (§2.3.9).

**Hyper-radiosensitivity (HRS) / induced repair (IRR).** Some cells show disproportionately high sensitivity per unit dose below ~0.2–0.5 Gy, attributed to a repair-induction threshold; effect is absent with high-LET radiation and has not translated into successful clinical fractionation strategies (§2.3.10, §3.9). First described by Lambin (1993) and Marples & Joiner (1993): maximal effect around 200 mGy, biologically equivalent to a dose 5–10× higher, seen across many tumour/normal cell lines including human skin, after both acute and fractionated dosing (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.2 §2.8.2.3; see [[concept_non-targeted-and-low-dose-effects]] for the broader family of low-dose phenomena this belongs to).

**Non-clonogenic assays** (apoptosis scoring, MTT, comet assay, micronucleus) are faster but generally correlate poorly with clonogenic survival over a wide dose range and are considered inferior predictors of true radiosensitivity except in cell types that die predominantly by apoptosis (§2.3.6, §3.8).

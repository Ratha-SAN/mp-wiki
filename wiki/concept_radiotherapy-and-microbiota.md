## Synthesis

**The human microbiota.** ~500 billion bacterial, viral, fungal, and archaeal cells inhabit the human body, the largest community in the digestive tract, which behaves functionally like an organ (intestinal homeostasis, nutrient transformation, epithelial barrier maintenance, immune system development) (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.5 §5.15.1). A healthy gut is dominated by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes (up to 80% combined), with Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, and Fusobacteria as minor phyla. The stable healthy state is termed eubiosis; disruption toward a pathogenic, low-diversity alternative stable state is dysbiosis — implicated (beyond radiotherapy) in inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic disease, and some neurological conditions.

**Pelvic radiotherapy reshapes gut microbiota composition.** Across at least eight prospective clinical studies, pelvic cancer patients (gynaecological, colorectal/rectal, prostate, lymph node, anal) show reduced fecal microbial diversity even before treatment, and pelvic RT further and consistently reduces diversity — Bacteroidetes tends to decrease and Fusobacteria increase during treatment, with Firmicutes reduced and Bacteroidetes/Fusobacteria/Proteobacteria/Actinobacteria enhanced after completion (Ch.5 §5.15.2). Reduced fecal microbial diversity during/after pelvic RT is consistently associated with intestinal radiotoxicity (enteropathy, enteritis, diarrhoea) (Ch.5 §5.15.3).

**Mechanistic and causal evidence (preclinical).** Rectal brachytherapy induces chronic post-exposure dysbiosis in animal models; irradiated-microbiota transplantation into germ-free mice transmits radiation-damage susceptibility, partly via epithelial IL-1 production (Gerassy-Vainberg et al., 2018) — direct evidence the microbiota is not merely a bystander marker but a causal contributor to radiation toxicity. Conversely, a 2020 *Science* study (Guo Hua et al.) identified "elite survivor" mice that tolerate high-dose total-body irradiation with reduced digestive toxicity, associated with specific bacterial families (Lachnospiraceae, Enterococcaceae) and metabolites (propionate, tryptophan-pathway products) that appear radioprotective (Ch.5 §5.15.3).

**Microbiota also modulates treatment efficacy, not just toxicity.** Commensal bacteria are required for efficient RT-induced antitumour immune activation (T-cell activation) — connecting to the abscopal-effect immunogenicity mechanism in [[concept_tumor-immunogenicity-and-abscopal-effect]] — though no specific bacterial taxa have yet been identified as responsible. Commensal fungi have an opposing effect: Shiao et al. showed specific fungi (Saccharomycetales order, *Candida albicans*) promote an immunosuppressive post-RT tumour microenvironment (affecting both T cells and macrophages), reducing treatment efficacy (Ch.5 §5.15.4).

**Clinical/translational implications.** Fecal microbial signature is proposed as a candidate biomarker for RT toxicity risk and efficacy prediction, though this needs further validation via multi-omics approaches (shotgun sequencing, metabolomics). Proposed interventions (not yet established standard of care) include high-fiber diet, probiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation to reduce RT-induced toxicity and/or improve efficacy (Ch.5 §5.15.5) (UNVERIFIED as clinical practice — source presents these as research directions, not established interventions).

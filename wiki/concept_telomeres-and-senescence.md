## Synthesis

**Telomeres.** Nucleoprotein caps (TTAGGG tandem repeats, 10–15 kb) at chromosome ends that (a) prevent the DNA-repair machinery from recognizing natural chromosome ends as double-strand breaks, and (b) count down the number of divisions a cell may undergo (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.3 §3.9.1). Telomeres shorten 50–200 bp per division (the DNA end-replication problem — DNA polymerase cannot fully copy the last base pairs of a linear template); after enough divisions the telomere reaches a critical length, the Hayflick limit, and the cell exits the cycle into senescence. Telomerase (a reverse transcriptase that extends telomeres) is active in germline and malignant cells but largely absent in somatic cells, which is why somatic cells senesce and malignant/germline cells do not (Ch.3 §3.9.1). Shorter telomere length correlates with greater radiation-induced cytogenetic damage (chromosome fusion, bridges, micronuclei) across lymphocytes, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, and cancer cell lines, making telomere length a candidate radiosensitivity marker and telomere-targeting a proposed radiosensitization strategy (Ch.3 §3.9.1).

**Senescence.** A prolonged, generally irreversible cell-cycle arrest triggered by extrinsic stressors (including radiation) or intrinsic physiological processes, distinct from quiescence (reversible), terminal differentiation, and cell death; its normal function is to remove potentially harmful cells from the proliferative pool (Ch.3 §3.9.2). Two main pathways initiate/maintain the arrest: p53–p21–Rb and p16INK4A–Rb. Senescent cells adopt a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) — a set of pro-inflammatory secreted factors — plus markers such as senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal). Radiation-induced premature senescence (p53-dependent) is a documented outcome of sublethal DNA damage, distinct from and sometimes alternative to apoptosis or mitotic catastrophe — see [[concept_cell-death-and-survival-curves]] for the other cell-death/arrest modes.

**Senescence's dual role in cancer.** Senescence acts as a tumour suppressor (halting proliferation of damaged/oncogene-activated cells, stimulating immune surveillance) but its SASP secretome can also promote tumorigenesis, proliferation, metastasis, and immunosuppression in surrounding tissue — meaning the accumulation of senescent cells after radiotherapy is not straightforwardly beneficial, and eliminating senescent cells ("senolytic" strategies) from tumours and surrounding normal tissue is an active adjuvant-therapy research direction (Ch.3 §3.9.2). This SASP-driven inflammatory signalling is a plausible additional mechanism (beyond the ROS/RNS pathway already described) contributing to normal-tissue late effects — see [[concept_normal-tissue-response]].

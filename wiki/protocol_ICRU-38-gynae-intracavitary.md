## Synthesis

**What this report is.** ICRU Report 38, *Dose and Volume Specification for Reporting Intracavitary Therapy in Gynecology* (1985), is the brachytherapy counterpart to [[protocol_ICRU-50-photon-beam-therapy]]'s external-beam framework, addressing cervix carcinoma intracavitary treatment specifically (MP Library/1985_@medphyslib_ICRU_Committee.pdf). It predates ICRU 50 by 8 years and explicitly cannot reuse that report's target/treatment-volume, single-reference-point approach, because the extreme dose gradient around a brachytherapy source (dose falls off continuously with distance from the source, with no flat "treatment volume" plateau as in external beam) makes a single specification point at best partially representative and at worst actively misleading.

**Historical systems this report reconciles.** Three pre-existing empirical dosing systems for cervix brachytherapy — the Stockholm system (fixed-dose-rate applicator loadings, Kottmeier 1964), the Paris system (radium tube geometry with a defined vaginal:uterine activity ratio, Lamarque & Coliez 1951), and the Manchester system (Paterson & Parker 1934, defining "point A" 2 cm lateral to the uterine canal and 2 cm superior to the mucosa, and "point B" 5 cm lateral to the midline) — each specify treatment differently, and point A/B's exact definition had drifted inconsistently between and even within centers over decades of use. The report's goal is a technique-independent reporting standard on top of, not replacing, these clinical systems.

**Why intracavitary dose specification differs fundamentally from external beam.** Because absorbed dose is maximal immediately adjacent to the sources and falls continuously with distance (rather than plateauing across a "treatment volume" as in external-beam box techniques), the size of the treated volume cannot be deduced by inspecting the isodose pattern the way it can in external beam — the radiotherapist must instead choose which isodose level defines treatment. Maximum, mean, median, and modal dose (all meaningful in external beam per ICRU 29/50) are explicitly judged not useful here; only a volume-based specification (the reference volume) is recommended.

**The 60 Gy pear-shaped reference volume.** For classical low-dose-rate cervix brachytherapy, 60 Gy is the accepted reference isodose level; the reference volume is the tissue volume enclosed by this isodose surface, described by three dimensions measured in two oblique planes containing the intrauterine source: height (dh, maximum dimension along the intrauterine source), width (dw, maximum dimension perpendicular to it, both in the oblique frontal plane), and thickness (dt, maximum dimension perpendicular to the source in the oblique sagittal plane). When intracavitary therapy is combined with external beam, the isodose level considered should be the 60 Gy level minus the external-beam contribution at the same location (e.g. 60 − 20 = 40 Gy if 20 Gy has already been delivered externally to the whole pelvis) — while the report explicitly cautions that combined dose does not necessarily produce the same biological effect as an equivalent single-modality dose, a caveat relevant to how [[technique_re-irradiation]]'s BED/EQD2-based cumulative dose comparisons should be read for brachytherapy-containing courses.

**Total Reference Air Kerma (TRAK).** Rather than the historical "mg-h" (milligram-hours of radium) convention — increasingly unworkable once radium was progressively replaced by ¹³⁷Cs, ¹⁹²Ir, and ⁶⁰Co sources of different specific activity — the report recommends specifying source strength via reference air kerma rate (kerma to air, in air, at 1 m, corrected for attenuation/scattering) integrated over treatment duration. TRAK is unambiguous across radionuclides, proportional to integral dose to the patient, and usable via the inverse-square law to estimate dose rate at points 20–10 cm from the source array with <4% error when the evaluation point is more than 2.5× the source array's largest dimension away.

**Reference points for organs at risk and bony structures.** Because clinically meaningful bladder/rectal dose cannot be derived from TRAK alone, the report defines explicit reference points: the bladder reference point (via a Foley catheter balloon filled with 7 cm³ contrast, pulled against the urethra) and the rectal reference point (5 mm posterior to the vaginal wall on a lateral radiograph). Two bony-landmark reference points are also defined for correlating brachytherapy dose with concurrent external-beam pelvic fields: the lymphatic trapezoid (constructed from S1-S2, L4, and symphysis landmarks, estimating dose to external/common iliac and para-aortic nodes) and the pelvic-wall reference point (representing dose to the distal parametrium/obturator nodes). These bony-structure points are specifically recommended as useful when intracavitary and external-beam therapy are combined — directly relevant to how OAR dose should be tracked across combined-modality or re-irradiation courses (see [[technique_re-irradiation]]).

**Dose-rate categories and the time-dose pattern.** The report distinguishes low dose rate (0.4–2 Gy/h, classical radium-era brachytherapy), medium dose rate (2–12 Gy/h), and high dose rate (>12 Gy/h, afterloading-enabled), noting these categorical boundaries are acknowledged as somewhat arbitrary. Because cell-survival dose-rate dependence in vitro is roughly dose-rate-independent above ~1 Gy/min and below ~1 Gy/h but varies in between, and because clinical experience accumulated over decades with low-dose-rate radium cannot be directly applied to medium/high-dose-rate afterloading without careful re-evaluation, the report insists application duration always be explicitly reported alongside dose — a caution that anticipates the general retreatment/altered-fractionation caution already present in [[concept_fractionation]] and [[technique_re-irradiation]], applied here to dose-rate rather than fraction size specifically.

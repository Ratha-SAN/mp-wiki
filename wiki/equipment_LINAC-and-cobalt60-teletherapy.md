## Synthesis

**What this source is.** WHO/IAEA, *Technical specifications of radiotherapy equipment for cancer treatment* (2021), is a joint interagency publication giving vendor-neutral technical specifications, safety measures, and QA recommendations for radiotherapy equipment, targeted at procurement decisions in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) (MP Library/Technical specifications of radiotherapy equipment for cancer treatment.pdf). It builds on an earlier 2008 IAEA equipment-specification publication and structures equipment into three procurement "packages" (Package 1: basic cobalt-60/single-energy LINAC service; Package 2: adds multi-energy LINAC with MLC; Package 3: adds IMRT/VMAT/IGRT/SRS/SBRT capability, not covered in detail here). This page covers the three EBRT beam-generating units: LINACs, cobalt-60 teletherapy units, and superficial/orthovoltage X-ray units.

**LINACs — the standard delivery platform.** The "conventional" LINAC design (open rotating gantry, isocentric rotation, bending magnet, flattened photon/electron beams, rotating collimator and couch) has been the EBRT standard since the 1970s; the report explicitly excludes "unconventional" designs (CyberKnife, TomoTherapy, Elekta Unity, ViewRay MRIdian, Varian Halcyon) from its scope. A Package 2 LINAC specification includes: 100 cm SAD isocentric gantry (±180° rotation, ≤2 mm mechanical isocentre diameter across all three rotation axes), 40×40 cm maximum field size at 50% isodose (minimum 4×4 cm), an MLC (≥80 motorized leaves, ≤1 cm leaf width at isocentre, <4% interleaf leakage, ≤1 mm leaf position accuracy), dual photon energies (6 MV standard plus a second energy — 10, 15, or 18 MV), four electron energies (commonly 6/9/12/15 MeV, penetration up to ~7 cm at 22 MeV per Table 4), and an amorphous-silicon electronic portal imaging device (EPID, ≥30×30 cm active area) replacing film/CR-based portal imaging. Beam symmetry ≤2% and flatness ≤3% (flatness F = (Dmax−Dmin)/(Dmax+Dmin) across the central 80% of beam width at 10 cm depth) are the specified tolerances.

**The second-photon-energy trade-off.** Higher photon energy reduces entrance dose for a given depth dose (Table 3: 6 MV gives 67.5% depth dose at 10 cm vs. 73.0% at 10 MV vs. 79.0% at 18 MV, all at 100 cm SSD) but increases exit dose — making higher energies more suitable for pelvis (large separation) than head-and-neck (small separation, where higher exit dose is a liability). High-energy beams (>8 MV) also generate neutrons in the accelerator head via (γ,n) reactions, requiring additional shielding (borated polyethylene) and producing short-lived activation products (e.g. aluminium-28, half-life 2.24 min, identified in a Varian Clinac) that motivate a brief staff-entry delay after high-energy irradiation. McGinley's cited data show neutron dose equivalent per unit X-ray dose rising from 0.02 mSv/Gy at 10 MV to 8.3 mSv/Gy at 18 MV, and 2–10× higher for IMRT delivery specifically — the report's stated reason IMRT/VMAT are generally not practised above 10 MV, and why the specification defaults to 10 MV as the second energy unless local need dictates otherwise.

**LINAC safety and QA.** Room design follows IAEA Safety Reports Series No. 47, NCRP Report No. 151, or ISO-16645:2016 for concrete shielding thickness (walls, floor, and ceiling, since the gantry rotates 360°); a maze reduces scattered-radiation shielding needs at the entrance at the cost of room footprint, versus a maze-less room requiring a heavy shielded motorized door. Standard safety features: beam-on/beam-ready illuminated signs, trefoil warning sign, facility access interlock, last-person-out button, audio-visual communication, emergency-off buttons (Figure 4). Reference dosimetry uses an absorbed-dose-to-water code of practice — the report names IAEA TRS-398 explicitly (see [[protocol_TRS-398-absorbed-dose-external-beam]]) — with QA program references to AAPM TG-142, AAPM Medical Physics Practice Guideline 8.a, and IPEM/European Commission equivalents. Total body irradiation (TBI) is noted as a distinct technique requiring an extended SSD (~4 m source-to-patient) and a treatment room with at least one wall 4 m from isocentre — flagged as high-risk and appropriate only with strong QA and departmental experience.

**Cobalt-60 teletherapy units — the resource-appropriate alternative.** Mechanically similar to a LINAC (rotating gantry/collimator/couch) but replacing the electron-gun/waveguide/target beam-generation chain with a sealed cobalt-60 source — lower purchase, running, and maintenance costs, and less infrastructure (no chilled water, three-phase power, or compressed air needed), at the cost of continuous source decay (requiring dose-rate-compensating longer treatment times as the source ages, and source replacement on a recommended 5-year cycle) and higher-activity radioactive-source security requirements. Available in 80 cm or 100 cm SAD configurations: the 80 cm unit gives higher dose rate for the same source activity (patient closer to source) at the cost of reduced isocentric clearance; departments procuring two units are advised to pair one of each SAD, since a decaying 100 cm-unit source can later be "cascaded" to the 80 cm unit for continued resource-sparing use. Source diameter (specified ≤2.5 cm, smaller optional at the cost of initial dose rate) directly determines beam penumbra width. Specified minimum output: 2.0 Gy/min to water at dmax, 100 cm SSD, 10×10 cm field, on delivery (74 GBq ±10% is the parallel figure given for the HDR brachytherapy cobalt-60 source in Chapter 4 — see [[equipment_HDR-brachytherapy-afterloader]] — teletherapy sources are far higher activity).

**Cobalt-60 safety, security, and QA.** Adds security-specific requirements beyond LINAC safety features: a permanent radiation area monitor warning if the source is in the open position, emergency-retract buttons, a manual T-bar source retraction mechanism, and — per ISO-2919 sealed-source classification — security features preventing unauthorized removal or sabotage of the radioactive material, not required for a LINAC (which has no radioactive source when beam-off). QA references parallel the LINAC section (IAEA 2008 guide, AAPM TG-40, European Commission Radiation Protection No. 162) with the same TRS-398-based reference dosimetry recommendation.

**Superficial and orthovoltage X-ray units — the low-energy alternative to electron beams.** Superficial units operate 50–100 kVp with short SSDs (5–15 cm) to maximize dose fall-off with depth (Table 13: at 2 mm Al HVL, depth dose falls to 46.0% by 2 cm depth); orthovoltage units extend this to 50–300 kV with longer SSDs (25–50 cm) for deeper treatment (Table 14: at 2 mm Cu HVL, depth dose is still 81.8% at 2 cm and 52.3% at 5 cm) — the report frames these as an alternative to LINAC electron beams for superficial disease (e.g. skin cancer), particularly relevant for departments with only a single-energy 6 MV LINAC lacking electron capability. Beam-on duration is controlled by a timer (superficial) or an internal monitor ionization chamber calibrated per energy (orthovoltage), with dual timer/chamber systems required to hold output within ±2% of standard reference dosimetry. Both are housed in lead-lined rooms (thickness per IAEA Safety Reports Series No. 47) rather than the concrete bunkers required for megavoltage units, reflecting the much lower shielding requirement at these energies. Reference dosimetry again points to TRS-398 or an air-kerma-based code of practice (IPEM, AAPM) as alternatives, with specialized additional dosimetry equipment (Farmer-type and plane-parallel chambers per IAEA TRS-398 Table 5, PMMA/aluminium/copper attenuator plate sets, pinhole applicator for focal-spot-size determination — Table 15).

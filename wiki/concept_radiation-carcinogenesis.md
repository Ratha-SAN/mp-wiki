## Synthesis

**Mechanism.** Carcinogenesis is modelled as a multi-step process — initiation, promotion, conversion, progression (raw/Radiation Biology- A Handbook for Teachers and Students.pdf, §4.4.1). Radiation-induced DNA damage preferentially causes deletions, favouring inactivation of tumour-suppressor genes as the presumed dominant mechanism of radiation-induced initiation (vs. oncogene activation, more associated with translocations) — see [[concept_dna-damage-and-repair]] and [[concept_cancer-molecular-biology]] (§4.4.1). No radiation-specific "fingerprint mutation" has been identified. Whether a dose threshold exists for cancer induction remains scientifically contested; ICRP (2007, §65) concludes the evidence cannot unambiguously validate or refute the linear-non-threshold (LNT) model at very low doses, and recommends against using LNT to calculate hypothetical case counts from trivial per-person doses spread over large populations (§4.4.1; see [[standard_ICRP-radiation-protection]]).

**A-bomb survivor Life Span Study (LSS) — the epidemiological gold standard.** ~120,000-person cohort (Hiroshima/Nagasaki), individually dosimetered, followed since 1949, still ongoing (§2.6.2, §4.4.3). Mortality analysis (Preston et al., 2003) attributed ~440 solid-cancer deaths (~4% of observed) and ~100 leukaemia deaths (~15%) to bomb radiation exposure among 44,771 deceased cohort members; significant dose-response found for stomach, colon, lung, leukaemia, breast, oesophagus, bladder, ovary, liver cancers — notably *not* prostate, cervix, or rectal cancer (§2.6.3). ICRP 60 (1990) estimated lifetime fatal cancer risk after acute 1 Gy exposure at ~10% (5% if delivered over weeks/months, 4% over a working life) (§2.6.3). The 2007 cancer-incidence analysis (17,448 solid cancers, Preston et al.) found overall incidence well-described by a no-threshold linear dose response down to <0.2 Gy, with 156 of the estimated excess cancers occurring in the 5–200 mGy range; oesophagus and bladder emerged as particularly radiosensitive (§4.4.3). ICRP 2007 recommendations are based, for the first time, on this incidence data rather than mortality data (§4.4.3).

**Other major epidemiological evidence.**
- *Chernobyl (1986):* 28 emergency-worker deaths from acute radiation syndrome; ~192,000 Russian liquidators studied (mean ~0.1 Gy external), no clear overall mortality increase through 1998 though later data suggest possible leukaemia increase; WHO (2006) attributed ~4.6% of deaths in the first year among the most exposed to radiation-associated disease. The dominant, well-established late effect is a childhood thyroid cancer epidemic (~5000 cases by 2002 in those <17 in 1986), linear no-threshold dose response, ~8-fold risk increase per Gy thyroid dose, driven by I-131 uptake via contaminated milk (§4.4.4).
- *Patients treated for benign disease:* ankylosing spondylitis patients (Court-Brown & Doll, 1957) showed a 10-fold increase in leukaemia deaths; post-partum mastitis and other benign-disease radiotherapy series (Shore et al.; Mattson et al.) established a linear dose-response for radiation-induced breast cancer, with risk strongly age-at-exposure dependent and negligible after menopause (§2.6.4.2, §4.4.5).
- *Radon:* first identified via Schneeberg/Jáchymov miners (lung cancer from inhaled radon progeny α-emitters); confirmed by uranium-miner cohort studies and case-control indoor-radon studies (largest: Wichmann et al., 2005, Germany); ~10% excess lung cancer risk at 100 Bq/m³; radon and smoking risks interact supra-additively/multiplicatively (§2.6.4.3, §4.4.6).
- *Radiation workers:* no clear excess in modern low-dose cohorts except pioneering-era workers (Sellafield, Mayak) (§2.6.4.1).

**Alternative view: LNT as a contested model, not settled science.** The synthesis above follows ICRP's own framing (uncertain at very low doses, but retained as a practical/precautionary regulatory tool). A named minority position within the field, represented in this wiki by Kardamakis, Baatout, Bourguignon, Foray & Socol ("raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf", Ch.1 §1.5), argues this framing understates how contested LNT actually is:
- LNT's origin is traced to H.J. Muller's 1946 Nobel lecture claiming "no escape from the conclusion that there is no threshold dose," a claim the authors call "ethically questionable" since Muller was reportedly already aware of counterevidence at the time (Ch.1 §1.5, citing Calabrese 2012). ICRP formally adopted LNT in 1956, replacing the prior tolerance-dose concept used since 1931.
- The authors state that evidence for LNT at very low doses is "inconclusive," that the model "has no indisputable scientific basis," and that despite this it "has acquired de facto the status of a scientific theory" and remains "the driving force of the prevailing radiophobia in the society" (Ch.1 §1.5) — language considerably stronger than ICRP's own "cannot unambiguously validate or refute" framing above.
- They point to hormesis (adaptive/beneficial cellular response to low-dose exposure) as an established phenomenon, while noting the human circumstances under which it occurs are not well characterized (Ch.1 §1.5).
- Countervailing point acknowledged even within this source: population-level epidemiological dose-response data (e.g. A-bomb LSS) "give consistency to the LNT relationship" for regulatory purposes, and the authors do not dispute that high-dose ionizing radiation causes cancer stochastically — the disagreement is specifically about low-dose extrapolation (Ch.1 §1.5).

This is presented here as a named alternative viewpoint, not as a correction to the ICRP-aligned synthesis above — the two sources disagree, and this page is not the place to adjudicate between them. See also [[concept_radiobiology-history]].

**Age and sex breakdown of LSS risk.** The pooled ERR/EAR estimates above vary substantially by age-at-exposure and by biological sex — see [[concept_age-and-sex-radiosensitivity]] for the disaggregated figures (e.g. solid-cancer ERR roughly 2× higher in women than men; children face longer latency but higher lifetime attributable risk).

**Second cancers after radiotherapy.** Now ~1 in 6 incident cancers in SEER registry data are second/higher-order malignancies (§3.16). For prostate cancer patients treated with RT, absolute second-cancer risk ≈0.3% (half in low-dose "out-of-field" regions via the LNT-type mechanism above, half in high-dose regions via chronic-inflammation-associated atrophy — a distinct, non-LNT mechanism) (§2.6.4.2, §3.16). Leukaemia risk after RT is roughly half that after chemotherapy and peaks 5–9 years post-exposure; risk scales with irradiated marrow volume (§3.16). Breast cancer is the most common second solid tumour in Hodgkin lymphoma survivors treated with chest RT at young age, with risk up to 8-fold at >40 Gy (§3.16). IMRT's larger low-dose volume and increased monitor units may increase second-cancer incidence, a particular concern in paediatric patients given their heightened radiosensitivity and longer life expectancy (§3.16).

**Important methodological caveat: ICRP's effective-dose risk coefficients should NOT be used to estimate radiotherapy second-cancer risk.** ICRP's effective-dose × risk-coefficient method (see [[standard_ICRP-radiation-protection]]) was derived for whole-body, low/uniform-dose exposure scenarios (chiefly the A-bomb LSS). Applied naively to radiotherapy's highly inhomogeneous dose distributions, it overestimates second-cancer risk by up to two orders of magnitude — ICRP itself (ICRP 60, ICRP 103) explicitly advises against using effective dose for risk estimation wherever peak doses exceed ~5 Gy with large inhomogeneities, i.e. essentially all radiotherapy fields (raw/Basic Clinical Radiobiology.pdf, Joiner & van der Kogel, 5th ed., Ch.27). Empirical cohort evidence confirms the mechanism: in a 66,541-patient cervical cancer cohort, the leukaemia risk per Gy of bone-marrow dose was less than 10% of the risk per Gy derived from the (whole-body, uniform) A-bomb survivor data — dose inhomogeneity itself appears to suppress carcinogenesis probability relative to a uniform-dose model, consistent with the direct-cell-sterilization competing-risk logic already familiar from [[concept_tumour-radiobiology]]'s TCP framework. ICRP's own recommendation is to use direct epidemiological cohort data (radiotherapy vs. surgery comparisons in cancers with high cure rates and long follow-up, e.g. cervix and prostate) rather than the effective-dose method for RT-specific second-cancer estimation (Ch.27).

**Statistical framing caveat.** Relative risk (RR) figures reported for RT-associated second cancers (e.g. very high RR for leukaemia after Hodgkin lymphoma treatment) can be clinically misleading in isolation: because many of the cancers with the highest RR are rare at baseline, even RR=4 may correspond to an excess absolute risk (EAR) of only ~6 cases per 10,000 patients per year. EAR, not RR, is the more clinically meaningful measure of second-malignancy burden (Ch.27).

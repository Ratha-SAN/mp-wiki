## Synthesis

**Overview.** Below roughly 0.5 Gy, several phenomena appear that a simple linear dose-response (whether targeted-DNA-damage or LNT-style) does not obviously predict: hyper-radiosensitivity, adaptive response, hormesis, and a family of "non-targeted effects" (NTE) occurring in cells that received no direct energy deposition at all but instead received signals from irradiated cells (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.2 §2.8). NTE dose-response saturates in the low-dose region — increasing dose beyond ~0.5 Gy produces no additional NTE (Ch.2 §2.8.3). This page is the mechanistic companion to the population-level LNT debate flagged in [[concept_radiation-carcinogenesis]] — the phenomena described here are the biological basis some researchers cite as reasons the low-dose region may not behave as a simple linear extrapolation from high-dose data would suggest, whatever position one takes on LNT's regulatory validity.

**Hyper-radiosensitivity (HRS) / induced radioresistance (IRR).** Already noted briefly in [[concept_cell-death-and-survival-curves]]; more detail here. First described by Lambin (1993) and Marples & Joiner (1993): below ~0.5 Gy acute dose (typically 100–800 mGy), survival curves show significantly more killing per unit dose than the higher-dose linear-quadratic fit would predict — increased chromosome breaks, micronuclei, unrepaired DSBs, gene mutations. Maximal HRS is generally seen at ~200 mGy, equivalent in biological effect to a dose 5–10× higher; the effect disappears above ~0.5 Gy (giving the "induced radioresistance" name for the transition) and is not seen with high-LET radiation (Ch.2 §2.8.2.3). Seen across many tumour and normal cell lines, including human skin, and after both acute and fractionated irradiation — relevant to radiotherapy and diagnostic/medical imaging dose ranges — but has not yet translated into a successful clinical fractionation strategy (Ch.2 §2.8.2.3).

**Mechanism of the HRS→IRR transition.** A 2003-discovered "early" G2 checkpoint (distinct from the longer-known, slower "Sinclair" G2 checkpoint) explains the transition: it is ATM-dependent and activates only once ATM autophosphorylation at Ser1981 crosses a threshold (detectable from 0.1 Gy, reaching >50% ATM phosphorylation around 0.5 Gy). Below ~0.3 Gy, cells irradiated in G2 proceed into mitosis with unrepaired damage (→ HRS); above that threshold, the checkpoint arrests them, giving time for repair (→ IRR), corresponding to roughly 8–10 DSBs (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.3 §3.20.1–3.20.2). The HRS/IRR transition can be permanently abolished by "priming" with a low dose (0.1–0.5 Gy) or by low-dose-rate exposure (0.1–0.3 Gy/h), via TGF-β3/iNOS induction (Ch.3 §3.20.2). HRS-proficient cells irradiated in the HRS dose range produce bystander signals reducing survival in unirradiated reporter cells; this bystander signalling stops once IRR dominates — bystander and HRS are mechanistically linked, not mutually exclusive as once thought (Ch.3 §3.20.3). Clinically, attempts to exploit HRS via hyperfractionation have not succeeded, but combining low-dose fractions with cell-cycle-synchronizing chemotherapy (e.g. paclitaxel to synchronize cells into G2) has shown promise in head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma (Ch.3 §3.20.4). HRS is not restricted to otherwise repair-proficient cells — individuals with hereditary DNA-repair-pathway mutations (AT/ATM, Fanconi anaemia, BRCA1/2, MRE11, DNA ligase IV — see [[concept_dna-damage-and-repair]]) show a distinct, independent form of radiation hypersensitivity (Ch.3 §3.20.1).

**Adaptive response.** A low priming dose can induce resistance to a subsequent larger dose; attributed to induced cellular/intercellular metabolic and repair changes rather than to selection of pre-resistant clones (Ch.2 §2.8.2.1). Reflects a broader shift in the field from a purely DNA-centric model of radiation damage toward a systemic, nonlinear model incorporating multi-level protection responses (Ch.2 §2.8.1). First described by Olivieri et al. (1984); most efficiently induced by priming doses of 0.01–0.5 Gy at dose rates 0.01–1.0 Gy/min, with challenge doses of 0.5–2 Gy, protective effect lasting ~3 cell generations; proposed molecular contributors include DNA repair, the NRF2 antioxidant pathway, NF-κB, MAPK signalling, autophagy, cell-cycle regulation, and bystander signalling — mechanism not fully resolved (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.3 §3.21.2).

**Radiation and inflammation/immunity (low-to-intermediate dose range).** Distinct from the hormesis/carcinogenesis debate but mechanistically adjacent: doses in the 0.1–1.0 Gy range measurably modulate immune-cell and endothelial behaviour — reduced TNF-α/IL-1β secretion by macrophages (0.3–0.7 Gy), reduced inducible nitric oxide synthase activity, reduced ROS in activated endothelial cells (strongest at 0.5 Gy), and osteoimmunological effects reducing osteoclast activation — the biological basis of low-dose radiotherapy already used clinically for benign chronic inflammatory/degenerative disease (e.g. arthritis) in parts of Europe (Ch.3 §3.15.2). The dose-response in this range is explicitly described as nonlinear (Ch.3 §3.15.2) — relevant context for, though not dispositive of, the LNT discussion in [[concept_radiation-carcinogenesis]].

**Membrane connections as a bystander-signalling route.** Beyond gap junctions and soluble/exosomal factors (see Bystander effects below), tunnelling nanotubes (TNTs, 50 nm–1.5 μm diameter, up to several cell-diameters long, F-actin/microtubule-based) and epithelial bridges (1–20 μm diameter, up to >1 mm long) provide direct long-range cytoplasmic connections between cells, transporting organelles (including mitochondria) and calcium/electrical signals; TNT network density increases under cellular stress, including radiation stress (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.3 §3.14).

**Hormesis.** A biphasic dose-response in which low doses of an agent harmful at high dose are instead beneficial — first described by Hugo Schulz (1884, toxic chemicals on yeast), term coined by John Ehrlich (1942) (Ch.2 §2.8.2.2). Radiation hormesis is the most thoroughly studied hormetic phenomenon. Two distinct senses of "low dose" are used in the literature and should not be conflated: radiation-protection usage treats "low dose" as ≤100 mGy, whereas in radiotherapy a 1000 mGy (1 Gy) dose might colloquially be called "low" relative to a 60 Gy course (Ch.2 §2.8.2.2). In the narrow sense relevant to the LNT debate, hormesis specifically means low radiation doses suppressing cancer incidence — not the broader claim that low-dose radiation treats other conditions (e.g. radon-spa treatment of arthritis, practiced in parts of Europe, is not "hormesis" in this narrow sense) (Ch.2 §2.8.2). See [[concept_radiation-carcinogenesis]] for how this bears on the LNT model specifically.

**Bystander effects (RIBE).** UNSCEAR-defined as a radiobiological effect transmitted from irradiated cells to neighbouring unirradiated cells, producing biological alterations (DNA damage, mutation, chromosomal aberration/instability, senescence, apoptosis, oncogenic transformation) in the receiving cells that can influence radiation-associated cancer risk estimates (Ch.2 §2.8.3.1). Mediated by soluble factors, gap-junction signalling, exosomes/miRNA, and cell-free chromatin released from apoptotic (not necrotic) irradiated cells; occurs over a few mm/cell diameters at the primary site; response magnitude is not necessarily proportional to the dose received by the irradiated cell, occurs even at low doses, and depends heavily on cell/tissue type and radiation quality (Ch.2 §2.8.3.1). Mechanism is still poorly understood ("in its infancy").

**Abscopal effects.** The in vivo, whole-organism analogue of bystander effects — radiation-like damage appearing in normal-tissue organs that were never irradiated, typically after high-dose partial-body radiotherapy, mediated by systemic factors (blood-borne signals, endocrine, immune/macrophage activation) (Ch.2 §2.8.3.2). Conceptually distinct from the tumour abscopal effect used in radio-immunotherapy (systemic antitumour immune response eliminating unirradiated metastases) — see [[concept_tumor-immunogenicity-and-abscopal-effect]] for that mechanism (cGAS-STING, DAMPs, dose/fractionation dependence).

**Clastogenic factors and genomic instability.** Clastogenic factors (CFs) — endogenous lipid peroxidation products, TNF-α, unusual nucleotides, other prooxidant chromosome-damaging species — appear in the plasma of irradiated subjects (therapeutic/accidental exposure, Chernobyl, A-bomb survivors, PUVA-treated psoriasis patients) and can induce chromosomal aberrations when co-cultured with unexposed donor cells; they are not specific to radiation exposure and occur in other oxidative-stress conditions, functioning more generally as oxidative-stress/carcinogenesis-risk biomarkers (Ch.2 §2.8.3.3). Genomic instability — non-clonal chromosomal damage or DNA change detected in the distant progeny of cells that were genetically normal at the first post-irradiation mitosis — and lethal mutations (permanently reduced plating efficiency in surviving progeny) are related NTE categories (Ch.2 Box 2.25).

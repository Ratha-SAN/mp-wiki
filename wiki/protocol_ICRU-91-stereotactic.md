## Synthesis

**What this report is.** ICRU Report 91, *Prescribing, Recording, and Reporting of Stereotactic Treatments with Small Photon Beams* (published 2017, Journal of the ICRU Vol. 14 No. 2, 2014), extends the [[protocol_ICRU-50-photon-beam-therapy]]/[[protocol_ICRU-62-photon-beam-therapy-supplement]]/[[protocol_ICRU-83-IMRT]] series to stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT) — an umbrella term this report defines to cover stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS, intracranial, single fraction), stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), and stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR), explicitly excluding intra-operative RT and HDR brachytherapy (MP Library/2017_@medphyslib_ICRU_Commitee_Prescribing.pdf). It is the ICRU counterpart most directly relevant to [[technique_SBRT]] and [[protocol_HyTEC-dose-constraints]], both already in this wiki.

**Why small fields need separate treatment: physics.** Small photon fields (as used in SRS/SBRT collimation) suffer three compounding dosimetric problems not present in conventional broad-field therapy: lack of lateral charged-particle equilibrium (secondary electron range can exceed field dimensions, especially at tissue-density interfaces like lung/bone), partial occlusion of the primary photon source by the collimation system (causing beam-penumbra overlap and reduced output), and strong detector-size/type dependence of the measured dose (conventional 10×10 cm² calibration protocols and standard ionization chambers are not directly valid). The report's response is a machine-specific reference field (msr) formalism — an intermediate calibration field, usually without a flattening filter, used because most modern small-field-capable machines cannot establish the conventional 10×10 cm² reference field — with a chamber-specific correction factor bridging msr to the actual clinical small field. Monte Carlo or other advanced model-based dose-calculation algorithms are recommended for SRT planning specifically to handle lateral electron scattering accurately in heterogeneous tissue (lung, bone).

**Historical context.** SRS traces to Lars Leksell's 1951 use of a stereotactic frame with a 200 kV x-ray unit to treat trigeminal neuralgia, and the 1968 Leksell Gamma Knife (179 cobalt-60 sources in a hemispherical array). Early treatments (pre-modern-imaging) were planned using pneumoencephalography and stereotactic atlases rather than direct tumor visualization — a striking contrast to the multimodal PET/MRI/CT GTV delineation now standard (see [[protocol_ICRU-83-IMRT]]'s GTV-imaging discussion). Linac-based SRS emerged in the early-to-mid 1980s; the non-invasive relocatable Gill-Thomas-Cosman frame (1991) enabled fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy rather than single-fraction-only treatment.

**Volume definitions largely inherited, with SRT-specific emphasis.** GTV/CTV/PTV/OAR/PRV/ITV/RVR are unchanged in principle from [[protocol_ICRU-83-IMRT]], but the report notes SRT-specific peculiarities: there may be no explicit GTV (e.g. a resection cavity PTV based directly on a CTV), CTV expansion beyond the GTV is "routinely outside the cranial vault" and a clinical choice case-by-case, and for non-tumor targets (trigeminal neuralgia, arteriovenous malformation) the CTV concept may not apply at all. Because SRT margins are tight (often <1 mm total geometric uncertainty in cranial SRS) but the consequence of a geometric miss is severe given steep dose falloff, the report stresses formal written imaging protocols per department.

**The reporting-level rule is stricter than for IMRT.** ICRU 83 permits Level 1 (point-dose) reporting for simple conventional treatments. This report explicitly states Level 1 reporting — including reporting absorbed dose at a single point — cannot be used for SRT at all, for five stated reasons: the intra-PTV dose distribution is inherently less homogeneous than in conventional RT (so a point can be misleadingly high or low), the point of maximum dose is unlikely to be representative, Monte Carlo dose in small voxels fluctuates statistically making a true point-max unreliable, PTV boundary dose gradients can exceed 10%/mm (so a small delivery shift changes the "reported" point dose substantially), and modern treatment-planning systems already provide adequate Level 2 DVH tools as standard. SRT reporting must therefore be Level 2 or Level 3.

**Dose-volume metrics, size-thresholded.** The report defines an SRT-specific pair of near-maximum/near-minimum metrics that switch definition based on PTV size — a genuine departure from ICRU 83's flat 2%/98% rule, motivated by the observation that a percentage-volume specification becomes meaningless once it falls below the dose-calculation grid resolution: for PTV ≥2 cm³, near-maximum = D2% and near-minimum = D98% (identical to ICRU 83); for PTV <2 cm³, near-maximum and near-minimum are defined by an absolute 35 mm³ volume element instead (D35mm³ and D(V−35mm³) respectively) — e.g. a small cranial lesion might use 15 mm³ voxels while a 100 mm³ volume element suits extracranial SRT, reflecting typical calculation-grid resolution differences between the two settings. D50% (PTV median dose) is always reported, and — a detail not generalizable outside lung — for peripheral lung lesions the dose to the target excluding uninvolved lung parenchyma, D50%(GTV/CTV), should be systematically reported as well, since tissue-density variation strongly distorts dose within lung tissue enclosed by the PTV.

**Prescription convention differs sharply from IMRT.** Historically SRS prescription used a "coverage isodose" — dose prescribed to an isodose surface (e.g. 50% or 60–80% of maximum) that best conforms to the PTV outline — rather than a percentage-volume-of-PTV target. The report explicitly declines to reverse this clinical convention (unlike its firm DVH-based recommendation elsewhere), instead recommending it be supplemented with the D50%/near-max/near-min metrics above so plans remain comparable across techniques. A direct consequence: ICRU 62/83's homogeneity constraint of confining PTV dose within 95–107% of prescription is explicitly not used in SRT — many clinicians deliberately prescribe a high central hotspot (dose to the 60–80% isodose line relative to maximum), particularly for brain metastases, where the report notes this practice has an established track record of good local control. This is a genuine, source-documented divergence in prescribing philosophy between conventional/IMRT and SRT, not a contradiction to be resolved — the two paradigms coexist for different clinical intents.

**Conformity metrics, revised.** ICRU 83's simple Conformity Index (Treated Volume ÷ PTV) is noted to be ambiguous when the prescription isodose volume both under- and over-covers the target simultaneously (worked example: PITV ratio of 2 can describe either uniform under-dosing or a mix of correctly- and severely over-dosed subvolumes with identical ratio). The report therefore favors the Paddick Conformity Index, CI = TVpiv² / (TV × PIV), which folds in target coverage explicitly rather than only isodose-to-target volume ratio. A Gradient Index (GI = PIVhalf / PIV, the ratio of the half-prescription isodose volume to the full prescription isodose volume) is introduced specifically for cranial radiosurgery, quantifying how sharply dose falls off outside the target — a metric with no ICRU-83 counterpart, reflecting the greater clinical weight placed on dose falloff steepness in cranial SRS specifically.

**OAR/PRV reporting.** Parallel-like structures: Dmean and/or Dmedian plus a clinically-correlated VD, as in [[protocol_ICRU-83-IMRT]]. Serial-like structures: near-maximum dose (D2% or D35mm³, matching the same size-dependent switch as PTV) plus dose to a small clinically relevant volume — absolute volumes are emphasized over relative/percentage volumes throughout this report specifically because SRT structures and margins are so small that percentage-based reporting can obscure clinically important absolute-volume detail.

**Level 3 (research) concepts.** Integral dose — total energy imparted across the whole irradiated volume — is flagged as worth recording particularly for benign-lesion SRT, given rising interest in second-malignancy induction risk from image guidance and low-dose scatter even though SRT's treated volumes themselves are small (see [[concept_radiation-carcinogenesis]] and the RVR discussion in [[protocol_ICRU-83-IMRT]]). Biology-based evaluation metrics (TCP/NTCP) are flagged with the same "useful but must be used cautiously" caveat as ICRU 83.

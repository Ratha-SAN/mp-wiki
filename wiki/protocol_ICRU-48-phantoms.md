## Synthesis

**What this report is.** ICRU Report 48, *Phantoms and Computational Models in Therapy, Diagnosis and Protection* (issued 15 June 1992), surveys both physical phantoms and mathematical/computational models used across radiotherapy, diagnostic imaging, radiation protection, and radiobiology (MP Library/ICRU_48_Phantoms_and_Computational_Models_in_Therapy,_Diagnosis.pdf). It sits alongside [[protocol_ICRU-44-tissue-substitutes]] as the second half of the phantom-materials picture: ICRU 44 asks what a phantom should be made of, this report asks what shape/structure it should take and how a computer can represent an entire patient without a physical phantom at all.

**Terminology, extending ICRU 44.** A *phantom* remains a material object (per [[protocol_ICRU-44-tissue-substitutes]]); a *computational model* is its mathematical/digital counterpart — a conceptual representation of an organ or body, generally expressed as equations or a voxel grid, used with a radiation transport code rather than physically irradiated. Phantoms and models are both further classed by function: *dosimetric* (absorbed-dose measurement/calculation at a specified geometry), *calibration* (establishing detector response; active calibration phantoms contain known radionuclide quantities, inactive ones do not), or *imaging* (image-quality assessment). Within each functional category, phantoms/models are further typed as *body* (human/part-human shape and composition, "anthropomorphic" when complex/realistic), *standard* (simple reproducible geometry, e.g. the ≥30 cm cubic water phantom already defined for radiotherapy dosimetry in [[protocol_ICRU-50-photon-beam-therapy]]'s era), or *reference* (used to define protection operational quantities, e.g. the 30 cm soft-tissue-composition sphere already defined in [[protocol_ICRU-44-tissue-substitutes]]).

**Historical arc.** Early 20th-century phantoms were tanks of water or blocks of wax (paraffin, beeswax, "Columbia wax"); wax was found in the 1930s to have photon attenuation deviating up to 48% from muscle/soft tissue at 20 keV, motivating high-atomic-number fillers (magnesium oxide, titanium dioxide) to correct low-energy response — an early, practical instance of the tissue-substitute matching problem later formalized in [[protocol_ICRU-44-tissue-substitutes]]. Realistic anthropomorphic phantoms emerged mid-century (Alderson "Rando" phantom, 1962: real human skeleton embedded in tissue-substitute body contours with slice-able sections for TLD placement; "Temex" system, 1961, similarly sliced). "Solid water" (WT1) is noted as increasingly displacing actual water tanks as the preferred standard dosimetric-phantom material by the time of this report, given its ±1% depth-dose match to water established in [[protocol_ICRU-44-tissue-substitutes]].

**Two distinct families of computational model.** *Mathematical models* represent organs as combinations of simple geometric surfaces (ellipsoids, elliptical cylinders, infinite slabs/slices) defined by quadric inequality expressions in Cartesian coordinates — the founding example being Fisher and Snyder's 1967/1968 adult model, extended into the well-known "ADAM"/"EVA" male/female models (Kramer et al. 1982) and pediatric "similitude phantoms" (produced by orthogonal scaling of the adult model's three axes, later refined using actual pediatric anatomical data rather than pure scaling once its limitations for some organs became apparent). *Tomographic models* instead use actual CT or MRI scan data from a real patient or cadaver, with each 3-D volume element (voxel) assigned to an organ by image segmentation — the first examples (Gibbs et al. 1984, dental radiology; extended to full-body radiotherapy/diagnostic/protection dose calculations by the late 1980s) used voxel resolutions from ~1.5 mm × 1.5 mm × 10 mm (adult) down to 0.85 mm × 0.85 mm × 4 mm (a 2-month-old baby cadaver model, ~1.42 million voxels over 4107 cm³).

**The trade-off between the two model types, as the report frames it.** Mathematical models are designed to represent a population average (ICRP Reference Man) via simple, computationally cheap idealized shapes — not any specific real individual — making them fast (executable Monte Carlo module ~400 kilobytes of memory) but anatomically approximate (bone marrow distributed homogeneously through the whole skeleton, no distinction between individual bones' actual marrow content). Tomographic models are built from a specific real patient's actual anatomy (more realistic organ shape, actual bone-marrow distribution resolvable to ~5–25 mm³ within the imaged skeleton) but represent only that one individual's proportions, and require far more computational memory (3–4 Megabytes for the transport-code input file) — a genuine, source-documented trade-off between population-representativeness and anatomical realism, not a simple hierarchy where one approach is strictly better. For individual-patient radiotherapy treatment planning specifically, the report is explicit that tomographic (i.e., the patient's own CT) models are the only suitable choice, since mathematical models by design cannot represent a specific patient's actual anatomy — directly consistent with modern CT-based treatment planning as the [[protocol_ICRU-50-photon-beam-therapy]]/[[protocol_ICRU-83-IMRT]] series already assumes as standard practice.

**Radiation transport codes.** Computational models require pairing with a radiation transport code to actually compute absorbed dose — either a discrete-ordinate numerical solution of the Boltzmann transport equation, or (much more widely used, given the difficulty of solving Boltzmann transport analytically in complex geometry) Monte Carlo simulation of individual particle histories. This is the direct computational-physics counterpart to the dose-calculation-accuracy discussion already present in [[protocol_ICRU-83-IMRT]] and [[protocol_ICRU-91-stereotactic]] (which recommends Monte Carlo specifically for small-field/heterogeneous-tissue SRT dose calculation).

**Animal phantoms in radiobiology.** A dedicated section addresses phantoms for whole-animal irradiation, since radiobiological dose-response findings (e.g., the spinal-cord tolerance studies in [[protocol_HyTEC-dose-constraints]] trace to animal radiobiology methodology of exactly this kind) depend on accurate animal dosimetry. The report gives elemental/water-lipid-protein composition tables for cat, mouse, pig, rabbit, and rat (for comparison against the human composition already tabulated in [[protocol_ICRU-44-tissue-substitutes]]), and recommends accuracy standards of better than 5% overall uncertainty and 2% precision for radiobiological dosimetry — tighter than the general ±30–50% protection-dosimetry tolerance in [[protocol_ICRU-44-tissue-substitutes]], reflecting radiobiology's need to resolve genuine small dose-response differences (a cited example: 10% absorbed-dose differences produced clearly observable differences in spinal-cord biological response in rats). Because animal size/species/strain vary too much for a single standard phantom to be broadly useful, the report notes phantoms in this field are usually purpose-built per experiment rather than standardized, with the European Late Effects Project (EULEP) mouse/rat phantoms being a rare standardized exception used specifically for inter-laboratory comparison.

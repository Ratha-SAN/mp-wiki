## Synthesis

**ICRP's four ethical values (ICRP Publication 138, 2018).** Underpinning the justification/optimization/dose-limitation system already described in [[standard_ICRP-radiation-protection]]: beneficence/non-maleficence (promote well-being, avoid harm — the ethical grounding for minimizing exposure and weighing costs/benefits, including who counts in that calculation, e.g. future generations, non-human life), justice (distributive — how risks/costs/benefits are allocated; and procedural — how decisions are made), dignity (respect for autonomy, self-determination, privacy, individual/community empowerment), and prudence (making discerning decisions without full knowledge of consequences — related to, but broader than, the precautionary principle from environmental ethics) (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.12 §12.3). ICRP also names transparency and accountability as procedural values, particularly for stakeholder engagement (Ch.12 §12.3). Some scholars have proposed adding empathy and honesty as further values, though this is not yet consensus (Ch.12 §12.3).

**Why risk acceptability isn't just about dose size.** The same absolute dose is judged very differently depending on context — medical X-ray exposure is broadly accepted; food irradiation (which delivers zero dose to the consumer) is often not (Ch.12 §12.6.1). Psychometric risk-perception research identifies the characteristics that drive this beyond magnitude/probability (Ch.12 §12.3.6, Table 12.2): voluntariness (workers voluntarily employed at a nuclear facility vs. involuntary public exposure), personal control (medical staff wearing a personal dosimeter feel more in control than an accident-affected public reliant on authorities/media), familiarity (long-term radon exposure becomes normalized, reducing remediation uptake despite unchanged risk), dread/catastrophic potential (a nuclear accident is perceived as more threatening than a transport accident with the same radionuclide), artificiality (natural background radiation is more readily accepted than human-made sources at equal dose), institutional trust, fairness/participation in decisions, and effect on children specifically. Personal control/autonomy connects directly to informed consent in medical ethics — one reason medical exposures are judged more acceptable is that they are voluntary and carry a clear personal benefit (Ch.12 §12.3.6.1).

**The "value-action gap."** Even when people have accurate risk perception, they often don't act on it — e.g. radon testing/remediation rates remain low even among people who rate radon risk highly and where mitigation cost is modest and often covered by health programs (Ch.12 §12.6.3). Health-behaviour models used in this field (Protection-Motivation Model, Health Belief Model, Theory of Planned Behavior, Transtheoretical Model, Extended Parallel Process Model) generally find that knowledge/risk perception alone is not the dominant predictor of protective behaviour — attitudes, perceived behavioural control, subjective/descriptive/moral norms, self-efficacy, and trust matter as much or more (Ch.12 §12.6.3, Table 12.3). This is directly relevant to designing effective patient/public communication, not just accurate risk disclosure.

**Principles for responsible risk communication.** Should be strategic (evidence-based, theory-informed), legitimate (a fair process for discussing the values/emotions at stake, not just the numbers), and ethically justified — the field has moved from treating risk communication as one-way technical education, to persuasive marketing, to today's socio-centric model built on public participation to bridge stakeholder gaps (Ch.12 §12.6.4). Practical guidance for communicating radiobiological findings to the public/media (adapted from Science Media Centre, 2012): give absolute risk alongside relative risk claims (state the outright cancer risk, not just "X% increase"), contextualize a new finding against prior evidence rather than presenting it in isolation, specify study size/type/stage (cell culture vs. animal vs. human trial) and a realistic technology timeline, distinguish correlation from causation explicitly, and separate findings from interpretation/extrapolation (Ch.12 §12.6.5). Radiobiology communication is judged to be unusually difficult because of genuine scientific disagreement at low doses (the LNT debate — see [[concept_radiation-carcinogenesis]]) and non-expert difficulty processing probabilistic information generally (Ch.12 §12.6.5).

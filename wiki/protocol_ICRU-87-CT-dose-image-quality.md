## Synthesis

**What this report is.** ICRU Report 87, *Radiation Dose and Image-Quality Assessment in Computed Tomography* (2012), is the dedicated CT-specific successor promised as "provisional" territory in [[protocol_ICRU-74-patient-dosimetry-imaging]], addressing both dose metrics and image-quality metrics (spatial resolution, noise) for modern multi-detector CT (MP Library/2012_@medphyslib_ICRU_Committee.pdf). The report's Preface motivates its scope with a striking statistic: CT accounts for roughly 49% of the radiation dose from medical imaging and, since medical imaging contributes ~48% of the average US citizen's total radiation dose, CT alone is responsible for approximately 24% of the entire population's radiation burden — despite CT scans being a minority of all imaging procedures performed. Over the 30 years preceding this report, average US population dose rose 72%, a large fraction attributed to increased CT utilization (multi-detector scanners enabling faster acquisition opened new clinical uses — cardiac CT, perfusion imaging, unsedated pediatric imaging — that further increased overall CT volume, over 100 million studies/year worldwide by the time of this report).

**Why CTDI-based dosimetry exists, and its central limitation.** The historical CT dose index (CTDI) family of metrics was built around the practical constraint that a radiation meter usable in CT needed to be longer than the narrow collimated beam width, since precisely positioning a small detector within a thin CT slice was difficult in the single-slice era — the standard tool became a 100 mm active-length "pencil" ionization chamber inserted into head (160 mm diameter) or body (320 mm diameter) PMMA cylindrical phantoms. CTDI∞ (the idealized, complete-scatter-tail integral of air kerma along the z-axis) is the conceptual reference quantity; CTDI_FDA (regulatory, ±7T integration limits) and CTDI100 (±50 mm limits, matching the practical 100 mm pencil chamber) are its measurable approximations. Weighted CTDI (CTDIw = ⅓·CTDI100,center + ⅔·CTDI100,periphery) approximates the average dose across the central scan plane, and volume CTDI (CTDIvol = CTDIw/pitch) accounts for the acquisition's actual table-translation pitch — CTDIvol is the value displayed on modern scanner consoles and is the closest thing to a single-number CT dose descriptor in routine clinical use. The report's central, explicitly-flagged limitation: CTDI100's fixed 100 mm integration length underestimates the true equilibrium dose (the dose that would be measured with a sufficiently long phantom capturing the full scatter tail) by a factor of roughly 0.6 on the central axis and roughly 0.8 at the periphery for the body-scan-length range (250 mm or more) typical of actual clinical abdomen/pelvis exams — meaning CTDIvol, exactly the number displayed on every clinical scanner, is a known systematic underestimate of true delivered dose for most real scans, not merely an approximation with unbiased error.

**Dose-Length Product (DLP) and effective dose estimation.** DLP = CTDIvol × scan length L (units mGy·cm) is a surrogate for total energy imparted to the patient (formerly called "integral dose") — because DLP scales with total scan length while CTDIvol does not, DLP better distinguishes, e.g., an abdomen-only exam from an abdomen+pelvis exam of the same per-slice technique. For comparing across scan types or against other imaging modalities, effective dose E can be approximated from DLP via region-specific conversion coefficients (E ≈ k × DLP, with k ranging from 0.0021 mSv/(mGy·cm) for head CT to 0.015 for abdomen/pelvis or trunk CT — nearly a 7-fold range, reflecting how much a given absorbed dose's stochastic-risk-weighted contribution depends on which radiosensitive organs are actually in the scan field). The report repeats, in CT-specific terms, the same caution already flagged in [[protocol_ICRU-74-patient-dosimetry-imaging]]: effective dose "was never intended to be used for the assessment of an individual patient's radiation dose, because the radiobiological weighting factors are not pertinent to a specific patient" — it is a comparison tool across protocols/populations, not a personalized risk estimate. The report's own worked example (thoracic CT) shows organ-by-organ absorbed dose and ICRP-103 tissue-weighting-factor contributions summing to a hypothetical effective dose of 9.3 mSv, dominated by lung (2.88 mSv contribution) and esophagus/thymus (1.08 mSv) despite bone marrow receiving the single highest absorbed organ dose (6.70 mGy) — illustrating that effective dose is driven by the product of dose and tissue radiosensitivity weight, not absorbed dose alone.

**CT's over-ranging problem.** Because helical/spiral CT's image-reconstruction algorithm requires projection data slightly beyond the nominal programmed scan boundaries, actual x-ray-on table travel exceeds the reconstructed image length — a phenomenon the report calls over-ranging, contributing up to ~40% additional dose-length product for short (~100 mm) scan lengths with wide beam collimation, dropping to ~7% for longer (300 mm) scans with narrow collimation. Newer scanners with adaptive/independent collimator systems substantially reduce this unneeded dose at scan-volume edges — a concrete example of how CT dose-reduction technique (see below) has evolved specifically in response to dosimetric findings of this kind.

**CT dose-reduction techniques (surveyed in this report).** Automatic exposure control (tube current modulated in real time based on patient attenuation, both angularly around the gantry rotation and longitudinally along the scan length) is now standard rather than a fixed technique-chart tube current; adjusting tube potential (kV) based on patient size, beam-shaping ("bow-tie") filters that reduce peripheral beam intensity to match the body's round cross-section, and modern iterative image-reconstruction/noise-reduction algorithms (which permit lower dose for equivalent diagnostic image noise than traditional filtered back-projection) are the other principal levers. This connects directly to the ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) principle the report cites as driving pediatric-CT-specific initiatives (Image Gently campaign) — pediatric patients are flagged as a particular concern given their smaller body habitus (relatively higher dose for a given technique factor), more rapidly proliferating/radiosensitive tissue, and greater remaining lifetime for radiation-induced cancer to manifest (see [[concept_age-and-sex-radiosensitivity]] and [[concept_radiation-carcinogenesis]]).

**Size-Specific Dose Estimate (SSDE) — the report's central methodological update.** Because CTDIvol is measured in a fixed-diameter reference phantom (160 or 320 mm) regardless of the actual patient's size, it systematically misrepresents dose for patients who differ substantially from those reference dimensions — a small patient receives more actual absorbed dose than CTDIvol suggests for a given technique, a large patient less. SSDE applies patient-size-derived correction factors (based on measured patient diameter or cross-sectional area from the scan itself) to CTDIvol to produce a more individually representative dose estimate, per AAPM Report 111 methodology that this report adopts and extends. This is the CT-dosimetry-specific instance of the general principle already seen in [[protocol_ICRU-48-phantoms]] and [[protocol_ICRU-74-patient-dosimetry-imaging]]: population-average reference-phantom dosimetry systematically misrepresents individual-patient dose, and patient-specific correction is preferred wherever practical.

**Image-quality metrics: spatial resolution and noise.** Beyond dose, the report devotes substantial treatment to modern CT image-quality assessment — modulation transfer function (MTF)-based spatial-resolution metrics (both axial-plane and along the z-axis, since modern isotropic-voxel CT has comparable resolution in all three dimensions, unlike older single-slice CT's much coarser z-resolution) and the noise-power spectrum (NPS), which characterizes not just noise magnitude but its spatial-frequency content — increasingly important because modern iterative reconstruction algorithms can reduce noise magnitude while changing its spatial-frequency texture in ways a single noise-magnitude number would miss. This is the direct CT-specific analogue of the general dose-vs-image-quality optimization theme already present in [[protocol_ICRU-74-patient-dosimetry-imaging]].

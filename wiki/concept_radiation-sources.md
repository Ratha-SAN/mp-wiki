## Synthesis

**Natural background radiation.** UNSCEAR identifies four foremost sources of public exposure to natural radiation (raw/Radiobiology Textbook.pdf, Ch.2 §2.2.1):
- *Terrestrial radiation* — primordial radionuclides (thorium, uranium, potassium) in the Earth's crust and building materials; levels vary substantially by location.
- *Inhalation* — radon (²²²Rn, from ²³⁸U decay) and thoron (²²⁰Rn, from thorium decay), inert gases that accumulate indoors when they escape through building foundation cracks; the dominant indoor natural-exposure pathway (see [[concept_radiation-carcinogenesis]] for the radon-lung-cancer epidemiology).
- *Ingestion* — food/water-borne radioisotopes with stable-isotope analogues (¹⁴C, ⁴⁰K) incorporated into the body via normal metabolism.
- *Cosmic radiation* — galactic cosmic rays (GCRs: ~87% protons, ~12% α-particles, 1–2% HZE heavy nuclei, from supernova remnants) and solar energetic particles (SEPs), both attenuated by atmosphere/altitude (e.g. Denver residents receive more cosmic dose than sea-level residents) and by Earth's magnetic field (Van Allen belts trap GCR/SEP secondaries; the South Atlantic Anomaly, ~200 km altitude over Brazil, exposes orbiting spacecraft to elevated flux). GCRs and SEPs are a major concern for spaceflight radiation protection, worsened for Mars (thin atmosphere, no global magnetic field) and lunar surface (no atmosphere at all) (Ch.2 §2.2.1).

**Artificial radiation sources** (Ch.2 §2.2.2): nuclear power plants (controlled fission, monitored low-level release of processed water/gas); TENORM — technologically enhanced naturally occurring radioactive material, e.g. coal ash (US coal-fired generation produced ~1100 t uranium + 2700 t thorium in ash in 2013), oil/gas, phosphate fertilizer, building materials; particle accelerators (LHC-scale and smaller — induced activation of beam-line components); radionuclide production facilities (reactors and ~1200 cyclotrons worldwide as of 2015, ~83 of 238 research reactors in 2017 used for isotope production, IAEA-regulated); hospitals (diagnostic radiology, nuclear medicine, radiotherapy — fluoroscopy is the largest contributor to staff dose, diagnostic imaging a minor one); ion radiotherapy facilities (proton/carbon; main radiation-protection issue is secondary neutron production); and nuclear weapons (uncontrolled fission, radioactive fallout — the Life Span Study cohort, see [[concept_radiation-carcinogenesis]], derives from Hiroshima/Nagasaki; weapons-testing fallout has contributed only minimally to overall background exposure compared to natural sources).

**Direct and indirect effects of radiation on the cell — physical/chemical staging.** Direct effects (ionization of a critical target molecule itself — DNA, lipid, protein) dominate for high-LET radiation (~2/3 of effect for alpha particles/neutrons); indirect effects (mediated by reactive oxygen/nitrogen species, ROS/RNS, from water radiolysis) dominate for low-LET radiation (~2/3 of effect for X-rays/gamma/beta) (Ch.2 §2.3, Box 2.9) — consistent with the direct/indirect action framework already in [[concept_radiation-interactions]]. The response unfolds in four stages: physical (<10⁻¹⁵ s, ionization/excitation), physicochemical (~10⁻¹² s, radical diffusion and ROS/RNS formation — H·, HO·, e⁻aq, O₂·⁻, H₂O₂, ONOO⁻ among the species formed), chemical (10⁻¹² s to seconds, radical reaction with DNA/lipid/protein), and biological (minutes to decades, depending on repair capacity, dose, dose rate, tissue radiosensitivity) (Ch.2 §2.3.2). Non-DNA targets are also damaged: mitochondria (>30% of cell volume; mtDNA strand breaks/deletions, cristae remodelling, respiratory-chain complex I/II/III sensitivity, increased ROS leakage), endoplasmic reticulum (dilation, degranulation), and Golgi apparatus (fragmentation) — with downstream effects on protein synthesis/maturation (Ch.2 §2.3.3).

## Synthesis

**What this page covers.** IAEA *Diagnostic Radiology Physics: A Handbook for Teachers and Students* (2014), Chapter 4 (Measures of Image Quality), §4.1-4.6.3. This is the mathematical framework — linear systems theory, contrast, unsharpness/MTF, noise, and signal detection — that later handbook chapters (receptors, fluoroscopy, mammography, CT, digital imaging) build on and reference repeatedly. Chapter 18 (Image Perception and Assessment — human observer models, ROC methodology) is closely related but not yet ingested; it will extend this page or form a companion page in a future pass.

**Linear systems theory (LSI) as the organizing framework.** An imaging system maps a scene f(x,y) to an image g(x,y) via a transfer function H. Two simplifying assumptions make this tractable: linearity (output is a weighted sum of input constituents) and shift invariance (the system response doesn't depend on position) — jointly "LSI." Under LSI, the general transfer integral reduces to a convolution g = h*f, and in the Fourier domain to simple multiplication g̃ = h̃f̃ — meaning a spatial frequency present in the object appears at the same frequency in the image, only with altered amplitude and phase. Real systems are rarely truly shift-invariant (edge/boundary pixels, oblique X-ray incidence) but are usually treatable as shift-invariant locally, or globally if blurring is small relative to image size. Digital systems are strictly only "cyclostationary" (statistics unchanged only for shifts by whole pixel multiples), not fully stationary — relevant whenever sub-pixel object position matters.

**Noise, stationarity, and ergodicity.** The image equation becomes ġ = Hf + ṅ once noise is included, with ṅ a single stochastic realization. A "wide sense stationary" process (only mean and covariance need be position-independent) is sufficient in practice; Gaussian and Poisson processes are each fully characterized by just mean/variance, which is why X-ray quantum noise (intrinsically Poisson) is commonly treated as Gaussian once counts exceed ~50/detector element. Ergodicity — where ensemble statistics can be obtained from a single realization by spatial averaging — is what makes noise measurement practical: instead of acquiring many images of the same scene to compute per-pixel variance, one image of a uniform region suffices. X-ray quantum noise is "white noise" (all spatial frequencies equally represented, uncorrelated between quanta) and is ergodic.

**Sampling theory and aliasing.** Digital detectors sample a continuous signal at points spaced by the sampling pitch a; the Nyquist frequency is 1/2a. If the object's spatial-frequency content exceeds the Nyquist frequency, the Fourier spectra of adjacent sampling replicates overlap — aliasing — which portrays real high-frequency content as false low-frequency content in the image. Aliasing is often unavoidable by design: e.g. amorphous-selenium digital radiography detectors have much higher intrinsic (photoconductor) spatial resolution than their pixel pitch supports, guaranteeing aliasing in the raw signal.

**Contrast — three definitions, three points in the imaging chain.** Weber (local) contrast C = (f_feature − f_background)/f_background, used for small features on a uniform background. Modulation (Michelson) contrast C_M = (f_max − f_min)/(f_max + f_min), used for patterns with comparable bright/dark area — this is the definition with Fourier-analysis relevance, since for a sinusoidal input the modulation contrast reduces to the simple amplitude-to-mean ratio B/A. Three distinct contrast stages are named: subject contrast (attenuation/uptake difference in the object itself — spectrum- and tissue-dependent for X-ray imaging, uptake/pharmacokinetics-dependent for nuclear medicine), image contrast (subject contrast as modified by the detector's own conversion/greyscale characteristics), and display contrast (image contrast as further modified by the display device and any processing). Film greyscale response is characterized by film gamma γ (typically 2.5-4.5) via the Hurter-Driffield curve; digital displays have a non-linear gamma typically 1.7-2.3.

**Unsharpness — PSF, LSF, ESF, and the MTF.** The point spread function (PSF) h(x,y) is the system's response to an impulse (point) input — directly the blurred image of a point object. The modulation transfer function (MTF), T(u,v) = |h̃(u,v)|/h̃(0,0), quantifies contrast degradation as a function of spatial frequency; T(0,0) = 1 by definition and 0 ≤ T ≤ 1 for well-behaved systems. The PSF/MTF pair is difficult to measure directly in 2-D, so practical measurement uses 1-D proxies related by transform pairs: the line spread function (LSF, from imaging a thin slit) is the Fourier-transform partner of the 1-D MTF; the edge spread function (ESF, from imaging a sharp edge, dE/dx = LSF) is preferred today because edges are easy to produce in any modality and the ESF method supports measuring the "presampling MTF" — the true system MTF including aperture blurring but excluding sampling-induced aliasing, obtained by imaging a slightly tilted edge and reconstructing a supersampled ESF from sub-pixel edge-to-pixel distances. Limiting spatial resolution (from bar/star patterns, in line pairs per unit length) is a related but distinct concept from the MTF (in cycles per unit length, sinusoidal basis) — as a rule of thumb, the limiting resolution for high-contrast objects occurs near MTF ≈ 0.05 (5%). For a cascaded imaging system (e.g. focal spot → detector → optics → camera), the overall MTF is simply the product of each component's MTF — a powerful tool for system design analysis, though aliasing after any sampling stage must be handled carefully since it breaks the simple multiplicative model.

**Noise power spectrum (NPS), DQE, and NEQ.** The noise power spectrum W(u,v) is the ensemble-averaged squared magnitude of the Fourier transform of the noise — the frequency-domain counterpart of the autocovariance function, related by an FT pair (Wiener-Khinchin-type relationship). Detective quantum efficiency DQE = SNR²_out/SNR²_in measures what fraction of the incident quanta's information (SNR) survives the imaging chain; for a perfect detector with quantum detection efficiency η and no added noise, DQE = η exactly. Generalized to spatial frequency, DQE(u,v) = Φ·Γ²·T²(u,v)/W(u,v), directly combining photon fluence Φ, greyscale gain Γ, the MTF T, and the NPS W — this single equation is why MTF and NPS measurements are the two pillars of modern detector characterization (elaborated further in the forthcoming Ch.7 receptor-technology ingest). Noise equivalent quanta NEQ = SNR²_out represents the "effective" quantum count the image is worth; DQE = NEQ/Φ. IEC standard measurement conditions specify RQA5 spectrum for general radiography and RQA-M for mammography.

**Signal detection and the Rose criterion.** The general definition SNR = ⟨g⟩/σ_g (inverse coefficient of variation) applies to quantum/photonic noise in a uniform region; a task-oriented alternative, SNR = |⟨x_a⟩ − ⟨x_b⟩|/σ, compares an object region (a) against background (b) using the background's standard deviation — also called signal-difference-to-noise ratio or contrast-to-noise ratio. Rose's empirical finding: an object is reliably distinguishable from background when SNR ≥ 5 (the Rose criterion), corresponding to a statistical error rate below 1 in 10⁶ — a strict threshold; some imaging tasks succeed at lower SNR. Wagner's taxonomy names four regimes limiting object detectability: quantum-noise-limited (ideal — detection is purely dose-limited), artefact-limited (e.g. CT/MRI reconstruction artefacts dominating), anatomy-limited (normal anatomic variation obscuring the signal), and observer-limited (human visual/cognitive limitations dominating) — well-designed systems aim to operate in the quantum-noise-limited regime, since that is the only regime where more dose reliably buys more detectability.

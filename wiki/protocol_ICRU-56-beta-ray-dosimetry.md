## Synthesis

**What this report is.** ICRU Report 56, *Dosimetry of External Beta Rays for Radiation Protection* (issued 5 January 1997), addresses beta-ray dosimetry specifically for radiation-protection purposes — external beta sources and skin/eye contamination — not therapeutic beta/electron beam dosimetry, which the report explicitly delegates to electron-beam therapy dosimetry methods instead (MP Library/1997_@medphyslib_ICRU_Commitee_Dosimetry.pdf). It is the radiation-protection counterpart to this wiki's therapy-focused ICRU pages, addressing a genuinely different problem: unlike therapeutic electron beams (well-defined parallel beams of known energy), beta radiation from contamination or unshielded sources has a highly variable and often unknown angular distribution and energy spectrum, requiring different measurement and calculation approaches entirely.

**Why beta dosimetry has historically lagged gamma dosimetry.** The report opens by noting that despite >40 years of routine beta-dose measurement, beta dosimetry accuracy and reliability have consistently lagged gamma-ray dosimetry — attributed partly to genuine technical measurement difficulty (see below) and partly to beta radiation's smaller typical contribution to overall occupational risk, since beta rays are comparatively easy to shield. This changes sharply in specific high-consequence scenarios: unshielded or lightly-shielded contamination can deliver beta doses more than 50 times the gamma dose from the same source, and the report cites Chernobyl as an example where beta-ray skin doses from surface contamination contributed substantially to worker deaths — motivating the report's emphasis on contamination and accidental-exposure scenarios specifically.

**Skin as the organ of primary concern, and why depth matters.** Because beta radiation is "weakly penetrating" (dose to skin/eye reaches the ICRP annual limit well before whole-body effective dose does), the skin and eye lens — not whole-body effective dose — are usually the binding constraint for beta exposures. The report adopts ICRP's specified reporting depth of 0.07 mm (70 μm, equivalently 7 mg/cm²) for skin dose, chosen because the radiosensitive basal layer of the epidermis (the target for stochastic cancer induction) sits at approximately 20–100 μm depth — the reporting depth is a compromise representing this layer, not the skin surface itself. Occupational skin dose (including hands/feet) is limited to 500 mSv/year; the eye lens has its own separate 150 mSv/year limit assessed at 3 mm depth, since normally-incident beta rays below ~1.7 MeV maximum energy do not reach the lens at all, making skin the binding constraint at lower energies and the eye becoming limiting only for higher-energy beta emitters. Testes have no separate ICRP dose-equivalent limit beyond the whole-body effective-dose-derived figure, but the report notes that skin dosimeters worn to control skin dose are, in practice, also adequately protective for testes given typical anatomical geometry.

**Operational quantities.** The ICRU's personal dose equivalent Hp(d) — dose equivalent in soft tissue at depth d below a specified point on the body — is used at d=0.07 mm (denoted Hp(0.07)) for skin and d=3 mm (Hp(3)) for the eye lens, both measured with a detector worn at the body surface under appropriate tissue-equivalent covering thickness. For area monitoring, the directional dose equivalent H′(d,Ω) is used rather than the ambient dose equivalent H*(10) used for strongly-penetrating radiation, since H*(10) (defined at 10 mm depth) is not an appropriate quantity for beta radiation, which rarely penetrates that deep.

**Why beta measurement is technically harder than gamma.** Three compounding difficulties, distinct from gamma-ray dosimetry: (1) beta dose varies sharply over very short distances due to strong self-attenuation within tissue, requiring measurement in an extremely thin detector layer matched to the target cell depth; (2) beta radiation scatters strongly in air, so its energy/angular distribution — and therefore the resulting dose — changes significantly with distance from the source, unlike a gamma source's more predictable inverse-square falloff; (3) many widely-used ionization-chamber survey instruments have poor energy and angular response for beta rays specifically, limiting their practical accuracy despite being adequate for gamma survey work. Extrapolation chambers — variable-electrode-spacing ionization chambers that measure ionization current as a function of chamber depth and extrapolate to zero thickness — are identified as the primary standard instruments for beta dosimetry precisely because they can approximate the required "effectively-thin" detector layer, though thermoluminescent dosimeters (TLDs) are the most common practical field/personnel dosimeter, using physically-thin or surface-sensitive detector configurations to approximate the same requirement.

**Calculation methods.** Most current beta-dose calculations rely on Monte Carlo simulation of electron transport (the ETRAN/Integrated TIGER Series and EGS families of codes are named as the principal tools) rather than analytical/deterministic methods, given the complexity of electron scattering and energy-loss physics — the same underlying electron-transport physics (collision/radiative stopping power, elastic scattering, energy-loss straggling) already touched on qualitatively in [[protocol_ICRU-91-stereotactic]]'s discussion of why small-field photon dosimetry also needs Monte Carlo methods, though the two reports address different radiation types and problems. The report tabulates extensive reference data: point-source dose distributions in infinite water medium, absorbed dose from surface (skin-contamination) sources, and dose from normally-incident broad beams, for common radionuclide beta spectra — these tables are the practical calculational backbone referenced by later radiation-protection software (e.g. the VARSKIN code, built directly on this report's point-source integration method).

**Special circumstances flagged.** Hot particles (small, highly radioactive discrete particles that can lodge on skin, e.g. from reactor accidents or radiopharmaceutical spills) require dedicated dosimetry techniques distinct from broad-beam/uniform-contamination methods, since their dose falls off extremely sharply with distance and standard area-averaged dose reporting can badly misrepresent the local peak dose. Mixed beta/gamma fields and mixed alpha/beta fields both require separating the dose contributions from each radiation type using distinct detector responses (Rβ vs Rγ) since a single dosimeter reading cannot generally disentangle them.

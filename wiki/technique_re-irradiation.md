## Synthesis

**Note on provenance.** Unlike the rest of this wiki's `raw/` sources, the eight papers cited here live in an external folder (`MP Library/`, not `raw/`) that the user pointed the assistant to mid-project. They are cited by filename below since they are not part of the wiki's own immutable `raw/` archive.

**Definition and classification.** Re-irradiation (reRT) is formally defined by the ESTRO-EORTC consensus as a new course of radiotherapy either to a previously irradiated volume (irrespective of toxicity concern) or one in which the cumulative dose to normal tissue raises toxicity concerns (MP Library/Re-irradiation in clinical practice....pdf, Willmann et al., 2023, citing Andratschke et al., Lancet Oncol 2022). Within this, "type 1" reRT has geometric overlap with the prior irradiated volume; "type 2" reRT has no volume overlap but cumulative-dose toxicity concern (e.g., lung after prior mediastinal RT); "repeat organ irradiation" describes a new course to a previously irradiated organ with neither volume overlap nor toxicity concern (MP Library/Prospective randomized clinical studies....pdf, Nieder et al., 2023).

**International patterns of care.** A 2022 cross-sectional survey of 371 physicians across 55 countries (endorsed by ESTRO/EORTC) found reRT is most commonly applied to the brain (77% of respondents), followed by pelvis (65%), head and neck (63%), thorax (60%), breast/chest wall (51%), and abdomen (39%). Prolonging local control was the near-universal treatment goal (90–96% across regions). Persistent grade ≥3 radiation-induced toxicity was the leading contraindication everywhere (77–80%), and a minimum interval of 6–12 months since prior RT was the most common threshold for considering reRT (45–55% of respondents). Institutional guidelines were scarce (available to only 16–19% of respondents per region), and cumulative organ-at-risk dose constraints varied widely between centers — most assume partial tissue recovery allows a higher cumulative dose than the initial-course constraint alone, but a minority apply the initial constraint cumulatively with no recovery credit (MP Library/Re-irradiation in clinical practice....pdf).

**Evidence base is thin.** Randomized controlled trials of reRT are rare. The clearest exceptions are two trials in recurrent nasopharyngeal cancer (surgery vs. IMRT reRT, favoring surgery on OS but with higher late-toxicity death rate in the reRT arm; and hyperfractionated vs. standard IMRT reRT, showing hyperfractionation reduces severe late toxicity and improves OS — see [[concept_fractionation]] for the general radiobiological rationale) and one trial in recurrent glioblastoma (bevacizumab alone vs. bevacizumab + reRT, PFS benefit but no OS benefit). For the majority of reRT indications identified in the patterns-of-care survey — prostate, rectal, cervical, non-small-cell lung cancer — no randomized trial exists at all (MP Library/Re-irradiation in clinical practice....pdf; MP Library/Prospective randomized clinical studies....pdf).

**Normal-tissue recovery.** The radiobiological case for assuming partial normal-tissue recovery between courses rests mostly on animal data: non-human primate, guinea pig, and rat spinal cord experiments show substantial recovery of sublethal damage, supporting the CNS dose-recovery assumptions used in nasopharyngeal reRT guidelines. By contrast, pig and mouse kidney experiments show no long-term recovery — recovery capacity is organ-specific and should not be assumed to generalize from the CNS literature (MP Library/Re-irradiation in clinical practice....pdf, citing Nieder & Langendijk). See [[concept_normal-tissue-response]] for the general late-effects framework this recovery assumption modifies.

**Prognostic themes recurring across disease sites.** Across the head-and-neck, cervical, and rectal reRT literature reviewed here, the same handful of factors recur as independent predictors of outcome: a longer interval between the two RT courses (thresholds of 6, 12, or 24 months depending on site and study), a higher re-irradiation dose (site-specific thresholds, e.g. >60 Gy for H&N, >50 Gy EQD2 α/β=10 for rectal), completion of the full planned reRT course, smaller recurrent tumor volume (GTV), and — where feasible — salvage surgery or concurrent chemotherapy. These are consistent enough across independently reported cohorts to be treated as general reRT principles rather than site-specific quirks.

**Head and neck re-irradiation.** Four papers cover this site in detail. IMRT-based reRT has clearly improved outcomes relative to the pre-IMRT era: the landmark RTOG 9610 and 9911 trials (altered fractionation, no IMRT) reported 2-year OS of only 15% and 25%; modern IMRT-era single- and multi-institution series report 2-year OS in the 33–59% range (MP Library/Re-irradiation for recurrent or second primary head and neck cancer.pdf, Lee et al., 2021, Radiat Oncol J). The Multi-Institution Reirradiation (MIRI) Collaborative's recursive-partitioning (RPA) classification — class I: >2 years since first RT with a resected tumor; class II: >2 years with unresected tumor, or ≤2 years without organ dysfunction; class III: ≤2 years with organ dysfunction (feeding tube/tracheostomy dependence) — has been externally validated and is a strong OS discriminator (2-year OS 87.5% vs. 51.8% vs. 0% in one validation cohort, p=0.008). In multivariate analysis across these studies, good performance status, interval ≥24 months, reRT dose >60 Gy, and salvage surgery independently predict OS and/or PFS; late grade ≥3 toxicity (osteoradionecrosis, bleeding, dysphagia, airway compromise) affects roughly 24% of patients, occasionally fatally (one treatment-related death from airway stricture/aspiration in a 42-patient cohort) (MP Library/Re-irradiation for recurrent or second primary head and neck cancer.pdf). A smaller, earlier (2013) Helical Tomotherapy series (n=9) supports the same direction of effect at a technique level — image-guided daily MVCT registration plus IMRT achieved 1-/2-year OS of 62.5%/50% with acceptable toxicity, consistent with the broader IMRT-era literature (MP Library/Re-irradiation of unresectable recurrent head and neck cancer....pdf, Jeong et al., 2013). A larger, more recent single-center cohort (n=48, Freiburg, 2010–2019) adds a dose-escalation angle specifically: patients who received a radiotherapy boost (simultaneous integrated or sequential, cumulative EQD2 ~122 Gy vs. ~119 Gy without boost) and those who completed the full prescribed course had significantly better OS; there was a trend toward improved OS with doses >50 Gy (p=0.080, not reaching significance at this sample size); no difference was found between cisplatin and cetuximab as the concurrent systemic agent (MP Library/The value of moderate dose escalation....pdf, Rühle et al., 2020). This dose-escalation finding echoes the general "radioresistant clonogen" rationale already in [[concept_tumour-radiobiology]] — recurrent tumor cells surviving a first RT course are plausibly enriched for radioresistant subclones, motivating higher reRT doses where normal-tissue constraints allow.

**Gynecologic (cervical) re-irradiation.** Two papers cover this site. Choice of modality depends heavily on the anatomic pattern of recurrence: high-dose-rate interstitial brachytherapy (HDR-ISBT) is preferred for central pelvic recurrence, while permanent radioactive seed implantation (PRSI, typically ¹²⁵I, "RISI") is preferred for peripheral/pelvic-sidewall recurrence — seeds placed centrally tend to fall out of the thin vaginal wall, whereas peripheral sidewall lesions sit far enough from organs at risk to tolerate a high local dose (MP Library/Re-Irradiation for Recurrent Cervical Cancer....pdf, Shen et al., 2022, Curr Oncol). Across HDR-ISBT series, complete response rates run 60–76% with 2-year local control and OS both typically >40–50%, though grade 3–4 toxicity (vaginal fistula, rectal bleeding, cystitis) is substantial — one series reported 33% grade 3–4 toxicity and 51% fistula rate. SBRT is increasingly used for lateral/pelvic-sidewall lesions not amenable to brachytherapy, with 2-year local control around 65–86% across small retrospective series, though gastrointestinal toxicity can be higher than with interstitial brachytherapy for non-nodal pelvic recurrence. A single-institution IMRT series (n=22, intrapelvic recurrence, 2006–2020) found that a longer interval between irradiations and a smaller GTV were independent favorable prognostic factors for local progression-free survival, and that GTV and treatment response were independent OS predictors; radiation dose showed a (borderline, p≈0.05) association with fistula formation, echoing the general dose-toxicity tradeoff seen in H&N and rectal reRT (MP Library/Re-Irradiation with Intensity-Modulated Radiation Therapy....pdf, Kang et al., 2023, Medicina). An emerging technical refinement is 3D-printed (coplanar or non-coplanar) template-assisted CT-guided seed implantation, which a meta-analysis found improves dose distribution and reduces operating time relative to free-hand implantation — a dosimetric/procedural advance rather than a new indication.

**Rectal/anorectal re-irradiation.** One paper (n=25, pelvic recurrence of anorectal cancer, 2005–2015) is notable for directly contradicting the dose-insensitivity finding of earlier rectal-reRT literature: reRT dose >50 Gy EQD2 (α/β=10) and use of concurrent chemotherapy at reRT were the only two independent predictors of both local progression-free survival and overall survival in multivariate analysis (3-year LPFS 58.3% vs. 0.00% above/below the 50 Gy threshold; 3-year OS 58.3% vs. 42.2%). Earlier studies using lower doses (~30–40 Gy, hyperfractionated) had found no dose-response relationship for local control — the authors of this later, higher-dose series suggest those earlier studies simply used doses too low to show an effect, consistent with the same "sufficient dose to overcome radioresistant clonogens" logic seen in H&N. Late toxicity was high in this cohort (52% of evaluable patients, grade 3 in 26% — chiefly fistula, bowel obstruction/perforation), materially higher than in more recent IMRT/SBRT series cited within the same paper (e.g., Youssef 2016: 3.2% grade ≥3 late toxicity at comparable doses), suggesting modern conformal techniques may reduce toxicity at a given reRT dose relative to the 3D-CRT-era technique mix used in this cohort (MP Library/Results of re-irradiation for pelvic recurrence in anorectal cancer patients.pdf, Park et al., 2019, Br J Radiol). See [[concept_fractionation]] for the BED/EQD2 formalism used throughout this literature to compare cumulative doses across differently-fractionated courses, and [[technique_SBRT]] for the related high-dose-per-fraction ablative approach increasingly used as a reRT modality in several of these sites.

**Cross-cutting toxicity note.** No genuine contradiction was found between these eight papers and the existing wiki content on normal-tissue tolerance or fractionation. The recurring pattern — higher reRT dose improves tumor control but also raises late-toxicity risk, with the balance point differing by organ and site — is consistent with, and adds clinical-outcome texture to, the general therapeutic-ratio and BED framework already established in [[concept_fractionation]] and [[concept_normal-tissue-response]].

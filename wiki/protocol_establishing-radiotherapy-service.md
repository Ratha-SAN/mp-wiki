## Synthesis

**What this page covers.** The programmatic (non-device-specific) chapters of WHO/IAEA's *Technical specifications of radiotherapy equipment for cancer treatment* (2021): Chapter 5, establishing a radiotherapy service (project management, procurement/maintenance/lifecycle, patient safety and quality management, staff education and training), and Chapter 6, emerging technology (MP Library/Technical specifications of radiotherapy equipment for cancer treatment.pdf). See [[equipment_LINAC-and-cobalt60-teletherapy]] and its companion equipment pages for the device-specific content this chapter sits alongside.

**Project management structure.** The report recommends four teams under an overall project management team: a clinical implementation team (establishes clinical protocols, multidisciplinary tumour boards, prepares patient-facing areas), a technology implementation team (equipment specification, bid evaluation, installation oversight, acceptance/commissioning, independent dosimetric audit, applications training, written policies, risk-based QC procedures), and a radiation safety implementation team (radiation management plan, licence applications, staff radiation monitoring, incident reporting system, pregnancy management procedures, radiation surveys). Establishing a radiotherapy service — from project start to first patient treated — is estimated at 18–24 months, decomposed into facility construction (~12 months), phased equipment installation (4–6 months), and major equipment acceptance/commissioning (4–6 months, itself dependent on equipment type/count and qualified-staff availability). Staff training lead time is flagged as a distinct, often longer-pole constraint: 4–5 years to train a radiation oncologist or clinically qualified medical physicist, 2–4 years for radiation therapists — meaning workforce development, not construction or procurement, is frequently the true critical path for a new service, particularly where staff must be trained rather than recruited already-qualified.

**Procurement guidance.** Beyond the device-specific technical specifications in Chapters 3–4, the report recommends tender/RFQ documents include supplemental information (site layout drawings, lead time, shipment method/route/incoterms, evidence of ISO/IEC compliance, regulatory clearance copies — FDA/CE/other stringent regulatory authority, a 10-year minimum spare-parts availability letter) and, post-award, import/customs documentation (certificate of origin, certificate of conformity, commercial invoice, waybill). A specific caution for countries without established medical-device regulatory regimes: both device manufacturer and specific make/model may need registration before import is legally possible, and in the absence of a registered product, an import waiver via the Ministry of Health is typically issued per-shipment only — meaning the underlying registration gap recurs for every future procurement unless a manufacturer completes full registration, which the report notes can itself take one to three years. Warranties should run a minimum of one year from the end of acceptance testing and explicitly cover preventive maintenance, updates, and upgrades, with maintenance-contract terms sought at the same time as the warranty is negotiated so lifecycle costs can be evaluated during vendor selection rather than after commitment.

**Maintenance and equipment lifecycle.** Maintenance contracts should specify: spare parts inclusion, safety/hardware/software update inclusion, service schedule (days/year, in-hours vs. out-of-hours), help-desk/remote-diagnostics access, whether major parts (e.g. a LINAC waveguide or CT X-ray tube) are included, and whether in-house maintenance engineers are permitted to perform front-line repairs — with uptime penalties (e.g. below a 95% target) and corrective-response-time penalties (e.g. 24-hour response, penalized per day of delay) as concrete contractual levers. Indicative equipment lifecycles (Table 19): LINAC and brachytherapy afterloader 10–15 years; cobalt-60 teletherapy and superficial/orthovoltage units minimum 15 years (source-exchange-dependent); CT simulator 5–10 years; conventional simulator and mould room equipment 10 years; TPS and OIS 5 years (extendable via ongoing software updates); patient immobilization equipment 5–10 years (usage-dependent); dosimetry/QC/radiation safety equipment 10 years; brachytherapy applicators only 3 years (usage-dependent); C-arm fluoroscopic unit 10 years; ultrasound unit 5–10 years. For sealed-source equipment, end-of-life decommissioning requires local radiation-regulator consultation and budgeted-for safe source disposal — and for high-energy LINACs specifically, potential head activation (see the neutron-activation discussion in [[equipment_LINAC-and-cobalt60-teletherapy]]) is an explicit decommissioning consideration, not just an operational one.

**Patient safety and quality management.** Grounds departmental practice in the International Basic Safety Standards' justification principle (weighing benefit against detriment for each medical exposure) and requires a radiation protection officer who chairs a radiation safety committee. Once operational, ongoing radiation-safety duties include managing an incident reporting and learning system, keeping radiation licences current, and ensuring physicist sign-off after any equipment repair/service before the unit returns to clinical use. The incident-reporting framework explicitly covers not just overdose but underdose and geometric miss (right dose, wrong place), under an explicitly no-blame reporting culture — directly consistent with the near-miss reporting obligation and "errors are system failures, not individual fault" framing already documented in [[standard_IAEA-medical-physics-ethics]]; the report names the IAEA SAFRON and ESTRO ROSEIS platforms as existing incident-reporting infrastructure. Quality management elements: chart check (process checks during planning/treatment), chart round (internal peer review of plans/prescriptions), double checks by different staff groups, equipment QC per Chapters 3–4, and periodic independent practice audits — the same practice-audit obligation documented from the medical-physicist-ethics angle in [[standard_IAEA-medical-physics-ethics]], here specified from the departmental-systems angle.

**Staff education and training (Table 20).** Four principal health professional roles, each with IAEA-published syllabi: radiation oncologist (medicine degree plus ≥3 years radiation oncology specialty training), clinically qualified medical physicist (postgraduate degree in medical physics plus 2–3 years structured clinical training under a qualified physicist's supervision), radiation therapist/RTT (radiation therapy degree, ≥1 year clinical training), and radiation oncology nurse (nursing degree plus 12–16 weeks supervised radiation oncology clinical training). National registration, tied to a professional code of conduct and ongoing professional-development requirements, is expected for all four roles — directly consonant with [[standard_IAEA-medical-physics-ethics]]'s continuous-professional-development recommendation for medical physicists specifically.

**Emerging technology (Chapter 6), assessed via WHO's health technology assessment framework** (cost-benefit, efficacy/performance evidence, implementation-process review, patient-safety review): *Electronic brachytherapy* — miniature X-ray tubes replacing sealed cobalt-60/iridium-192 sources, avoiding both scheduled source-exchange logistics and sealed-source security/regulatory burden (see [[equipment_HDR-brachytherapy-afterloader]]), but with efficacy evidence still limited to early cervical-cancer studies and tube-reliability/cost-effectiveness (if short-interval tube replacement is needed) still unproven. *Image-guided and adaptive radiotherapy* — Table 21 catalogues the current imaging-modality landscape: MRI and PET-CT for simulation-stage delineation (cervix/prostate and lung/head-and-neck respectively), in-room MRI/kV planar/kV cone-beam CT/MV imaging (including dedicated low-energy 2.5 MV portal beams for improved image quality) for pre-treatment verification and adaptive replanning, active/inert fiducial markers (gold seeds, some RF-emitting for real-time tracking) for tumour localization, and 4DCT/optical surface imaging for respiratory-motion management and gating — real-time imaging immediately before treatment is framed as serving a dual role, both enabling adaptive replanning and functioning as an independent safety check that the target is actually covered by the planned beams. *Hypofractionated and stereotactic radiotherapy on conventional LINACs* — historically the domain of dedicated units (frame-based systems, Gamma Knife), now increasingly deliverable on conventional LINACs given ≤5 mm MLC resolution, FFF beams (>10 Gy/min dose rate reducing overall treatment time), and six-degrees-of-freedom couches (added pitch/roll beyond the standard three translations plus yaw) — see [[technique_SBRT]] and [[protocol_ICRU-91-stereotactic]] for the radiobiological and dose-reporting side of this trend; this report notes smaller/multiple targets and higher dose per fraction demand more careful dosimetry and geometric placement, consistent with [[protocol_ICRU-91-stereotactic]]'s stricter (Level 2/3-only) reporting requirement for SRT.

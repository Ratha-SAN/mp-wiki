## Synthesis

**What this report is.** IAEA Technical Reports Series No. 277, *Absorbed Dose Determination in Photon and Electron Beams: An International Code of Practice* (1987, prepared by Andreo, Cunningham, Hohlfeld and Svensson), was the first internationally unified code of practice for converting an ionization-chamber reading into absorbed dose to water for external-beam radiotherapy, superseding a patchwork of incompatible national protocols (MP Library/AbsorDoseDeterPhotonElectronBeams_TRS277.pdf). It is now superseded in turn by [[protocol_TRS-398-absorbed-dose-external-beam]], but its air-kerma-based calibration formalism remains the historical reference point for understanding pre-2000s reference-dosimetry literature and calibration records, and its underlying physics (Bragg-Gray cavity theory, perturbation corrections, beam-quality specification) is still the conceptual basis of the modern protocol.

**Institutional context: the SSDL Network.** The report grew out of the IAEA/WHO Secondary Standard Dosimetry Laboratory (SSDL) Network, established 1976 (following an IAEA absorbed-dose calorimeter built in 1960 and a mail-based ⁶⁰Co dose-intercomparison service begun in 1966) to bridge the gap between Primary Standard Dosimetry Laboratories (PSDLs, of which only ~20 existed worldwide) and individual hospitals — by 1987 the Network comprised ~60 laboratories, 46 in developing countries. This code of practice was explicitly commissioned to give SSDL-served hospitals worldwide a single, unambiguous calibration procedure rather than each following a different national protocol.

**Scope.** Covers low/soft X-rays (tube potential <100 kV), medium/conventional X-rays (>100 kV), high-energy photon beams (mean energy ≥0.66 MeV, i.e. ⁶⁰Co and accelerator megavoltage beams), and high-energy electron beams (5–50 MeV, with some data extending below 5 MeV). Does not cover absorbed dose to media other than water directly.

**Basic quantities.** Absorbed dose D = dε̄/dm (Gy); kerma K = dEtr/dm (Gy, energy transferred to charged particles by uncharged radiation); exposure X = dQ/dm (C/kg, air-specific, photon-specific, ionization-based — related to air kerma by X = Kair(1−g)/(W/e), where g is the bremsstrahlung-loss fraction and W/e is energy per ion pair per unit charge). The report explicitly flags exposure as a legacy quantity being phased out in favour of air kerma, retained here only because some standards laboratories still calibrated in exposure and much existing reference literature used it — directly consistent with the formal quantity definitions later codified in [[protocol_ICRU-60-quantities-and-units]] (published a decade later).

**W/e value.** The mean energy expended per ion pair in dry air, W/e, is central to the whole calibration chain (it converts measured ionization charge to absorbed dose to air via the Bragg-Gray equation). This report recommends **W/e = 33.97 J/C**, an update from the ICRU's earlier 33.85 J/C value, adopted per a 1986 CCEMRI(I) recommendation reflecting revised graphite stopping-power calculations — the same constant, updated once more since, that is the subject of [[protocol_ICRU-31-W-value]] (which gives 33.85 eV as its own headline figure, from the earlier 1979 ICRU 31 report) — flagged here as a genuine, source-documented revision over time rather than a contradiction between the two wiki pages, since TRS-277 explicitly describes updating away from the ICRU-31-era value.

**Beam quality specification — genuinely different physics for electrons vs. photons.** For **electron beams**, quality is specified via the mean energy at the phantom surface, Ē₀, and/or the most probable energy, Ep,0 — both derived empirically from measured range parameters (practical range Rp, half-value depth R50) rather than measured directly, using empirical energy-range relations (Ep,0 = C1 + C2Rp + C3Rp², with C1=0.22 MeV, C2=1.98 MeV/cm, C3=0.0025 MeV/cm²; Ē0 = C4·R50, C4=2.33 MeV/cm). For **high-energy photon beams**, a single energy parameter is explicitly rejected as inadequate (unlike gamma sources, accelerator bremsstrahlung spectra can't be characterized by nominal accelerating potential alone — this was insufficient even to specify depth-dose data reliably); instead the report adopts the tissue-phantom ratio TPR20,10 — the ratio of absorbed dose at 20 cm and 10 cm depth in water at a fixed source-chamber distance, 10×10 cm field — as the practical, directly-measurable beam-quality index that stopping-power ratios and other dosimetric parameters are tabulated against. Low/medium-energy X-rays are instead specified by tube potential, total filtration, and half-value layer (HVL) in aluminium (≤100 kV) or copper (≥50 kV, with overlap 50–100 kV).

**The calibration chain: PSDL → SSDL → user.** A traceable measurement chain from primary absorbed-dose-to-graphite/exposure/air-kerma standards (PSDLs, cross-checked to agree within 0.2–0.3% at ⁶⁰Co quality) through SSDLs (which calibrate a user's chamber and issue a calibration factor NK = Kair,c/Mc, at ⁶⁰Co quality) to the hospital user. The user's ionization chamber, calibrated only at the SSDL's ⁶⁰Co reference quality, must then be used to determine absorbed dose to water at the actual (possibly very different) clinical beam quality — the entire point of the code of practice's formalism is bridging that gap via the Bragg-Gray cavity equation and a chain of correction factors, on the explicit assumption that the absorbed-dose-to-air calibration factor ND determined at calibration quality remains valid at the user's clinical quality (justified here by treating Wuser/Wcalibration ≈ 1, i.e. assuming this ratio's energy dependence, estimated at ≤1.5%, is negligible — an approximation later scrutinized further in TRS-398).

**Core formalism (Bragg-Gray cavity equation).** Absorbed dose to water at the effective point of measurement: Dw(Peff) = M·ND·(sw,air)u·pu, where M is the corrected chamber reading, ND is the absorbed-dose-to-air chamber factor (derived from the SSDL's air-kerma calibration factor NK via ND = NK(1−g)·katt·km, correcting for bremsstrahlung loss, chamber-wall attenuation/scatter, and non-air-equivalence of chamber wall material), (sw,air)u is the water-to-air mass collision stopping-power ratio at the user's beam quality, and pu is the perturbation correction factor. This is the same Bragg-Gray cavity-theory relationship already introduced generally in [[protocol_ICRU-31-W-value]]'s discussion of ionization-chamber calibration physics, here worked out into a complete, tabulated clinical procedure.

**Perturbation correction and effective point of measurement.** Because inserting an ionization chamber into water locally perturbs the electron fluence (chamber wall material differs from water; the air cavity itself displaces water and alters local electron scattering), two corrections are needed: the perturbation factor pu, and a shift of the nominal measurement point to an "effective point of measurement" Peff displaced upstream from the chamber's geometric centre — recommended displacements (as a fraction of the cylindrical chamber's cavity radius r): 0.5r for electron beams and ⁶⁰Co gamma rays, 0.75r for high-energy photon beams, 0.35r for ¹³⁷Cs, and zero for medium X-rays. Because electron scattering power rises sharply as electron energy falls, this perturbation effect grows more severe at lower electron energies, motivating a preference for plane-parallel chambers (whose effective point of measurement sits at the front face of the air cavity) over cylindrical chambers for lower-energy electron beams — the smaller the cavity, the smaller the perturbation and gradient-correction problem.

**Uncertainty and quality framework.** The report explicitly frames its accuracy target against ICRU's clinical recommendation of ±5% (or better) delivered-dose accuracy, requiring dosimetry uncertainty tighter still, since dose delivery accuracy also depends on treatment-planning and delivery-chain uncertainties beyond calibration alone — the same accuracy-budget logic later generalized by the graduated Level 1/2/3 dose-reporting standards in [[protocol_ICRU-50-photon-beam-therapy]] and successors. Appendix A adopts the BIPM's (then-emerging, now-standard) statistical framework for combining measurement uncertainties.
